# Brixton Black Women's Group

*An autonomous Black women's group formed in Brixton in 1973 or 1974, largely by women from the Black Power organisations; Olive Morris was a founder member, and the group published Speak Out, helped set up OWAAD and initiated the Brixton Defence Campaign after 1981.*

The Brixton Black Women's Group was, in the words of a member quoted in *The Heart of the Race*, "the first autonomous Black women's group of its kind, certainly in London".[^1] It brought together women who had been active in London's Black political organisations, the [Black Panther Movement](../Organisations/ORG-0031.md) among them. It took up questions that those organisations had left aside, such as women's work, economic dependence and childcare.[^2] [Olive Morris](../People/PER-0025.md) was one of its founder members.[^3]

## Founding and founders

A founder member recalls in *The Heart of the Race*: "We formed the Black Women's Group in 1973. We didn't even bother with a name."[^2] The Lambeth Archives catalogue and Liz Obi date the group's founding to 1974.[^4][^5] According to the same recollection, the founders "came mainly out of Black organisations", which were by then "in the process of disintegrating". Before the group was formed, women had been meeting in caucuses within those organisations.[^2] The authors of *The Heart of the Race* describe these caucuses within the Black Power movement as the ground from which the Black women's movement grew.[^6] Beverley Bryan, one of the book's three authors, recalls that she was in the Black Panther movement before the Brixton group, and in the Brixton group before [OWAAD](../Organisations/ORG-0033.md).[^7]

Named founder members include:

- **[Olive Morris](../People/PER-0025.md)**, "one of the founder members", who had been in the Black Panther movement.[^3][^8]
- **Gerlin Bean**, "a founding member", according to the list of interviewees in *Do You Remember Olive Morris?*.[^9]

## Aims

The founding member quoted above explains that the group wanted to put Black women's issues "at the top of the agenda for the first time". It chose not to join the white women's movement, whose priorities, such as abortion and wages for housework, seemed to members "middle-class preoccupations". They were more concerned with pay for Black women working outside the home, for example as night cleaners, and with childcare.[^2] The same member says that they "never were" anti-men, and would not at first have called themselves feminists.[^1] Later, an issue of the group's journal *Speak Out* was "largely devoted to the issue of Black feminism", as members worked to redefine the term for themselves.[^10] *The Heart of the Race* credits Morris with helping the group develop "a coherent political ideology" grounded in the needs of ordinary Black people and linked to anti-imperialist struggles.[^8]

## Activities and campaigns

- **Brixton.** The group helped to set up and run "the first Black bookshop in Brixton". It joined the Railton 4 Campaign against police harassment, and it organised against disruptive units in schools and for parental rights.[^1] Morris also helped set up Sabarr Bookshop. The Lambeth Archives catalogue places it at [121 Railton Road](../Places/PLC-0081.md) and calls it "the second black bookshop to open in Brixton".[^8][^4]
- **International links.** Members kept links with the African Students Union, African women's study groups, and the ZANU Women's League.[^1][^11]
- **OWAAD.** The group was represented at the Warwick meeting of February 1978 from which [OWAAD](../Organisations/ORG-0033.md) was formed, and contributed "a lot to" it in its early years.[^11][^12] After Morris returned to Brixton in 1978, other women in the group took up the work she had begun in Manchester to launch OWAAD.[^13] The group's own account, published in 1984, describes a demonstration in central London against state harassment organised by women from AWAZ, the Asian Women's Movement, and the Brixton Black Women's Group.[^14]
- **The Brixton Defence Campaign.** After the Brixton uprising of 1981, a member recalls, "the women's group came together with a few brothers in the community" to plan a defence campaign, and "we initiated the Brixton Defence Campaign". Its first meeting was held at the Black Women's Centre, which became its base. The campaign found legal representation for those arrested and publicised police tactics through street meetings on Railton Road.[^15]
- **Remembering Olive Morris.** After Morris's death in 1979, the group organised memorial services at the Abeng Centre. It was involved in the dedication of 18 Brixton Hill as Olive Morris House in 1986, according to the Lambeth Archives catalogue.[^4] The Olive Morris Collection also holds an invitation to an evening of remembrance that the group organised on the anniversary of her death.[^16]

## Publications

The group's newsletter was *Speak Out*, sometimes printed as *Speak Out!*.[^10][^17] Its first issue carried Morris's report on her visit to China.[^16][^17] Bryan recalls that the group wrote "with no names", as a collective, a practice she traces back to the Black Panther movement.[^7]

## Meeting places

The sources consulted name the Black Women's Centre as the base of the Brixton Defence Campaign in 1981.[^15] The group's 1984 article gives the address of the Brixton Black Women's Centre as 41 Stockwell Green, SW9. It describes the Centre's welfare-rights advice, health group, meeting rooms, small library and holiday projects for children.[^14] A list of Black women's groups compiled for *The Heart of the Race* in 1985 gives "Brixton Black Women's Centre and newsletter Speak Out" at 41a Stockwell Green, as a "Meeting place for several Black women's groups".[^18]

## Fate

The sources consulted do not record when the group ceased to meet. It was still active in 1984, when members published their account of OWAAD in *Feminist Review*.[^14] In 1985 the Brixton Black Women's Centre and *Speak Out* were still listed at Stockwell Green.[^18]

## Evidence notes

- **Founding year.** *The Heart of the Race* gives 1973, the year recalled by a founder member.[^2] The Lambeth Archives catalogue and Liz Obi give 1974.[^4][^5]
- **The bookshops.** The member quoted in *The Heart of the Race* speaks of "the first Black bookshop in Brixton". The Lambeth Archives catalogue calls Sabarr "the second black bookshop to open in Brixton".[^1][^4] The sources do not establish whether these refer to the same shop.
- **The Brixton Defence Campaign.** The member's account does not name her group in the passage itself; *The Heart of the Race* indexes these pages under the Brixton Black Women's Group.[^19]
- **Spelling.** *The Heart of the Race* spells the bookshop "Sarbarr"; the Lambeth Archives catalogue spells it "Sabarr".[^8][^4]
- **Stockwell Green address.** The sources give three numbers: 41 (the 1984 article), 41a (the 1985 list of groups) and 45 (the address given for *Speak Out* in the book's list of periodicals).[^14][^18][^20]
- **Meeting place.** Ashley Whitfield suggests that the group met in a building on Railton Road with a bookshop on its ground floor, but writes conditionally and gives no number.[^21] The address of the group's meetings before 1981 is not established in the sources consulted.
- **Membership.** No membership list or total is given in the sources consulted. Other named members, such as Judith Lockhart, Melba Wilson and Jocelyn Wolfe, are known from the interviewee list in *Do You Remember Olive Morris?*.[^9]
