# Organisation of Women of African and Asian Descent

*The Organisation of Women of African and Asian Descent (OWAAD), a national network of Black women's groups formed in February 1978 after a meeting at Warwick University; it held national conferences from 1979, published FOWAAD, and broke up in 1982 or 1983.*

<!-- MEDIA: image slot — the front cover of FOWAAD no. 1 (1979), held in the Olive Morris Collection at Lambeth Archives; to be selected and added by the editor -->

The Organisation of Women of African and Asian Descent (OWAAD) was the first national network of Black women's organisations in Britain. *The Heart of the Race* calls it one of "the most decisive influences on Black women's politics in this country". It notes that OWAAD's "lifetime spanned only five years".[^1] It was based in London, and its national conferences, day schools and newsletter, *FOWAAD*, brought together Black women from across the country.[^1][^2] [Olive Morris](../People/PER-0025.md) was among its founders, and the [Brixton Black Women's Group](../Organisations/ORG-0032.md) took part from the start.[^3][^4]

## Founding and founders

OWAAD was formed in February 1978. One of the founders, quoted in *The Heart of the Race*, recalls that "a group of about fifteen of us met at Warwick University". They had met to discuss how to bring more women into the African Students Union.[^4] The women present represented groups including the ZANU Women's League, the Ethiopian and Eritrean Women's Study Groups, the Black Women's Alliance of South Africa and the Brixton Black Women's Group. When African women at the meeting rejected the idea of a women's caucus within the union, the group decided "to go it alone" as an autonomous organisation.[^4] The Brixton group's own account of 1984 likewise dates the launch to February 1978, by African women active in the African Students' Union (UK).[^5]

At first OWAAD saw itself as a "sister" organisation to the union. A month later its members lobbied the union's annual general meeting in Manchester. They won a donation of £50 for publicity, and the account credits Olive Morris with winning the day.[^6]

Founder members named in the sources consulted include:

- **[Olive Morris](../People/PER-0025.md)**, "a founding member of OWAAD", according to Altair Roelants. *The Heart of the Race* writes that she began work in Manchester to launch OWAAD, which women in the Brixton group took up after she returned to Brixton in 1978.[^3][^7]
- **Stella Dadzie**, "one of OWAAD's founder members" and a co-author of *The Heart of the Race*.[^8]
- **Jocelyn Wolfe**, of the Brixton Black Women's Group, "a founding member of OWAAD".[^9]

## Name and membership

OWAAD began with African and Afro-Caribbean women, and then adopted a position of Afro-Asian unity, changing its name accordingly.[^4] *The Heart of the Race* explains that Asian women who joined argued "very strongly that we should be organising jointly. That's how the name got changed".[^6] Afro-Caribbean women remained the majority, and how to organise across both communities "became one of OWAAD's most controversial discussion points".[^4] Many African members eventually "drifted away and went back into the national support groups".[^6]

## Aims and structure

The founders aimed "to create and promote a national network of Black women".[^10] Roelants quotes a 1979 issue of *Spare Rib*, which described OWAAD as "a national black women's umbrella organisation made up of local (autonomous) black women's groups and individual sisters".[^3] By the end of its second year it coordinated groups and individuals across London. The national network remained "very informal", and London stayed the focus of most activity.[^2] OWAAD tried to build a "non-hierarchical" structure, with representation rotated to allow for childcare and other commitments.[^2]

## Conferences

OWAAD held national conferences every year from 1979 to 1982.[^1] The first, in 1979, was in the words of *The Heart of the Race* "the first national Black women's conference to be held in this country".[^6][^11] A participant remembers that "it must have been several hundred of us". Many women went home to form local Black women's groups, some of which outlived OWAAD.[^11] In Haringey, for example, women who had attended the first conference set up the Haringey Black Pressure Group on Education.[^11] The Brixton group's account dates the second conference to 1980, the third to 1981 and the fourth to 1982. It calls the fourth "inevitably a debacle".[^5]

## Campaigns

*The Heart of the Race* lists campaigns in which OWAAD women took part:[^12]

- organising against virginity testing at Heathrow Airport;
- opposition to ethnic record-keeping and secret files;
- support on the picket lines for Asian women on strike at Futters in 1978;
- a campaign against the racism and sexism of the 1979 White Paper on immigration and nationality;
- defence campaigns for those arrested at Southall in April 1979, when Blair Peach was killed;
- community campaigns after the killing of Akthar Ali and the death of Richard Campbell in July 1980.

The Brixton group's account records that OWAAD "organised a sit-in protest and picket at Heathrow Airport" against virginity tests.[^5]

## Publications

OWAAD's newsletter was *FOWAAD*.[^1] The first issue appeared in July 1979. Its back page was a tribute, "In Memory of Olive Morris", who had died that month.[^3] The Olive Morris Collection at Lambeth Archives holds a copy of issue 1, donated by Gerlin Bean.[^13] In 1985 *The Heart of the Race* listed *FOWAAD* among periodicals "no longer in circulation".[^14]

## Division and fate

*The Heart of the Race* traces OWAAD's break-up to differences over the relevance of feminism to Black women's struggle. "It was because of the difficulties of coming to terms with these implications that the rifts in OWAAD began to emerge. They would eventually lead to OWAAD's demise."[^15] The Brixton group's account of 1984 points to the difficulty of holding together so many different elements without a shared political grounding, and to political questions that the organisation could neither properly discuss nor resolve. It concludes that, despite attempts at revival, "the organisation is in fact now dead".[^5]

## Evidence notes

- **Date of the name change.** *The Heart of the Race* says that OWAAD adopted Afro-Asian unity "six months later, in August 1978". The Brixton group's account says that "It was not until the Winter of 1978" that OWAAD took its new name.[^4][^5]
- **First conference.** *The Heart of the Race* gives an attendance of three hundred, and the Brixton group's account 250.[^6][^5] The date, place and exact wording of the first conference's report are not legible in the copy of *The Heart of the Race* consulted. The 1984 account does not give them either.
- **End.** *The Heart of the Race* gives OWAAD's lifetime as 1978 to 1983. The Brixton group's account, written in 1984, refers to "its demise in 1982".[^1][^5]
- **Original name.** The sources consulted do not establish, in a legible passage, the name OWAAD used before the change.
- **Secondary quotation.** The *Spare Rib* description is known here from Roelants's quotation and was not consulted directly.
