# African National Congress

*The South African liberation movement founded in 1912, banned in South Africa from 1960 to 1990; its external mission under Oliver Tambo made London one of its bases, with a headquarters at 28 Penton Street, Islington, that South African agents bombed in 1982.*

<!-- MEDIA: image slot — photograph of the ANC office at 28 Penton Street, Islington, or of an ANC delegation in London; to be selected and added by the editor -->

The African National Congress (ANC) was founded in South Africa in 1912. This profile concerns its London history. After the South African government banned it in 1960, its leaders abroad built an external mission, and London became one of its bases. [Oliver Tambo](../People/PER-0020.md) led the movement from exile, and his family lived in London. [Nelson Mandela](../People/PER-0002.md) visited the city secretly in 1962. In 1982 the ANC's London headquarters at [28 Penton Street](../Places/PLC-0068.md) was bombed by South African agents.[^1][^2][^3]

## Founding and founders

The organisation was launched as the South African Native National Congress on 8 January 1912. Callinicos quotes Pixley ka Isaka Seme's call for unity at the launch: "We are one people."[^4] Tambo gave a speech every year on 8 January, the anniversary of the founding meeting.[^5] In *Long Walk to Freedom* Mandela recalls Gaur Radebe telling him that the ANC was "the oldest national African organization in the country, having been founded in 1912".[^6]

## Aims and early contacts with Britain

Callinicos traces a long tradition of ANC diplomacy. In 1912 its leaders petitioned the new South African government against the coming Land Act. In 1914 they sent a delegation to Britain to urge that the old "qualified" Cape franchise be extended to all South Africans. In 1918 another delegation went to the Peace Conference in Paris.[^7]

## The external mission and London

The ANC and the Pan Africanist Congress were declared illegal on 8 April 1960, in the crackdown that followed the shootings at Sharpeville.[^8][^9] Callinicos writes that Sharpeville hastened a plan for an external mission, and that Tambo had to leave at once.[^9] He flew on to London, where he stayed with John and Diana Collins. His wife Adelaide and their children reached London on 15 September 1960.[^10]

The ANC kept a London office, which the sources consulted record mainly through its staff and leadership:

- **Chief representatives.** Raymond Mazisi Kunene was chief representative in London. After the ANC's consultative conference at Morogoro in April 1969, Reg September was appointed in his place.[^11] Callinicos also names Mendi Msimang among the "highly educated" staff of the office.[^12]
- **Tensions.** Callinicos describes resentments in the London office in the late 1960s, which "coincided with racial identity". A group of African members complained that the office had lost its "African image" once ANC membership was opened to all races.[^13][^11]
- **The Penton Street headquarters.** By 1982 the ANC's headquarters in Britain was at 28 Penton Street, Islington.[^1] The Anti-Apartheid Movement's archives committee recorded that Tambo, when in London, "was known ... to hold early morning meetings at the ANC Mission in Penton Street".[^3]

## Campaigns in Britain

Callinicos describes close links between the ANC and the Anti-Apartheid Movement. The movement's "Release Political Prisoners" campaign, the sports boycott, its cultural projects and the campaign for economic sanctions were "conceived along ANC lines". ANC members "did much AAM legwork", and ANC-supporting students helped establish its branches at universities and polytechnics.[^12] In June 1963 Raymond Kunene spoke for the ANC at a London meeting of the [Committee of Afro-Asian Caribbean Organisations](../Organisations/ORG-0025.md) in support of the civil rights movement in the United States.[^14]

## Events in London

- **[Mandela's visit of 1962](../Events/EVT-0005.md).** In June 1962 Mandela spent about ten days in London on ANC business. He saw Tambo and met British political leaders.[^15]
- **[The bombing of 28 Penton Street](../Events/EVT-0021.md).** On Sunday 14 March 1982 a bomb exploded against the rear wall of the headquarters. Nine former South African security agents later admitted responsibility to the Truth and Reconciliation Commission.[^1] The ANC later returned to the rebuilt building.[^1]
- **[The address in Westminster Hall](../Events/EVT-0006.md).** In July 1996 Mandela, by then President of South Africa, addressed both Houses of Parliament.[^16]

## Publications

The ANC printed material at Penton Street. The 1982 bomb threw its printing press through a wall, but the ANC printer Patric Tariq Mellet recalled that it "was repairable and was soon set to work again".[^1] The titles printed there are not recorded in the sources consulted.

## Leaders and links with people in the atlas

- **[Oliver Tambo](../People/PER-0020.md)** was sent abroad in 1960 to build the external mission. He became acting president after Chief Albert Luthuli's death in 1967, and was later elected President.[^9][^17]
- **[Nelson Mandela](../People/PER-0002.md)** visited London for the ANC in 1962. He became the first democratically elected President of South Africa in 1994.[^15][^18]

## Fate

On 2 February 1990 President F. W. de Klerk announced the lifting of the bans on the ANC, the PAC, the South African Communist Party and other organisations.[^19] Tambo returned to South Africa in December 1990, after three decades in exile.[^20]

## Evidence notes

- **Earlier London offices.** The sources consulted do not give the addresses of the ANC's London office before Penton Street, or the date it moved there.
- **The London office's work.** Apart from the bombing, the sources consulted say little about day-to-day activity at Penton Street.
