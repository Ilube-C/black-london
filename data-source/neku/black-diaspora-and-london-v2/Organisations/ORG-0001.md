# African Association

*Black-led association founded in London in 1897 or 1898 by Henry Sylvester Williams to lobby for Africans and people of African descent in the British Empire; it called the 1900 Pan-African Conference and merged into the Pan-African Association.*

The African Association was a London society of people of African descent that lobbied the British government on the affairs of Africa and the Caribbean. Its founder and secretary was the Trinidad-raised law student [Henry Sylvester Williams](../People/PER-0003.md).[^1][^2] Adi and Sherwood date the beginning of the organised Pan-African movement to its founding.[^3] Within three years it had called the [First Pan-African Conference](../Events/EVT-0001.md), into whose new Pan-African Association it was then merged.[^1]

## Founding

The sources differ on the year. Fryer dates the founding to 1897, the year of Queen Victoria's Diamond Jubilee, and Clarke to September 1897. Adi and Sherwood write that Williams founded it in 1898, after contacting Black people around the country, "apparently mainly university students".[^1][^4][^2] Adi and Sherwood add that full membership was restricted to people of African descent, with others admitted as associate members.[^2]

## Aims

Fryer quotes the Association's objects:

> To encourage a feeling of unity to facilitate friendly intercourse among Africans in general; to promote and protect the interests of all subjects claiming African descent, wholly or in part, in British Colonies and other places, especially in Africa, by circulating accurate information on all subjects affecting their rights and privileges as subjects of the British Empire, by direct appeals to the Imperial and Local Governments.[^1]

## Leadership and members

Williams was secretary. The president was the Revd Henry Mason Joseph, an Antiguan, and the treasurer was Mrs E. V. Kinloch, a woman from Natal married to a Scotsman. Kinloch campaigned with Williams against the treatment of Black people in South Africa before returning home in 1898.[^1] At a meeting of members and friends on 11 January 1898, Williams said that the Association wished to be as representative as possible, "so that its information shall be direct and first hand from the various parts of the Empire". He proposed branch societies in the colonies.[^1]

## Meetings and campaigns

The Aborigines' Protection Society's journal reported in March 1898 that "frequent meetings of its increasing number of members are held". Fryer, quoting that journal, calls the Association in effect "an African and West Indian lobby".[^1] In March 1898 it sent the Colonial Secretary, Joseph Chamberlain, a memorial "on the distress in the West Indies". Adi and Sherwood write that it called for changes in land tenure and education and criticised the agricultural monopolies that impoverished small farmers.[^1][^2] Seven months later it petitioned Chamberlain to safeguard "the vital interests, customary laws and welfare" of Africans in the new Rhodesian constitution.[^1][^2]

On 19 March 1898 an Association meeting decided to issue a circular announcing a conference in two years' time.[^5] A week before the conference, it presented an engrossed memorial to the departing Bishop James Johnson of Lagos. The memorial urged Black people to develop "our own chroniclers" and "our own libraries and organizations".[^5]

## Where it met

The sources consulted do not record where the Association held its meetings or whether it had an office. Its gathering of delegates on 6 July 1900 was held at the Reform Club.[^5]

## Fate

At the close of the 1900 Conference "the African Association was merged into a new Pan-African Association".[^5] Williams was its general secretary and [Samuel Coleridge-Taylor](../People/PER-0010.md) sat on its executive.[^5][^6] Adi and Sherwood record that while Williams was abroad some London officers declared the Pan-African Association defunct. Supported mainly by students and transient residents, it "collapsed some time in 1902–3".[^6]
