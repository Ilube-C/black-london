# League of Coloured Peoples (LCP)

*The League of Coloured Peoples, founded in London on 13 March 1931 by Dr Harold Moody, who led it until his death in 1947; a welfare and pressure group that published The Keys, campaigned against the colour bar and drafted a "Charter for Coloured Peoples" in 1944.*

The League of Coloured Peoples (LCP) was founded at the [Central YMCA](../Places/PLC-0040.md), Tottenham Court Road, on 13 March 1931 (see [Founding meeting of the LCP](../Events/EVT-0017.md)).[^1][^2] For the last sixteen years of his life its president was [Harold Moody](../People/PER-0012.md), the Jamaican-born doctor of Peckham. Fryer calls it "the first effective black pressure group in this country".[^1] A historian quoted by Fryer sums it up as "social club, housing bureau, pressure group, investigative agency and employment agency".[^1]

## Founders and aims

The League grew out of Moody's years of practical help to Black people facing the colour bar in lodgings and work. It was formed with the encouragement of the visiting African American historian Dr Charles Wesley.[^1] Its first four aims, printed in each issue of its journal, were to promote its members' "Social, Educational, Economic and Political Interests", to "interest members in the Welfare of Coloured Peoples in all parts of the World", "to improve relations between the Races" and to co-operate with sympathetic bodies. A fifth, added in 1937, offered financial help to "coloured people in distress".[^1]

## Leadership and members

Moody was president from 1931 until his death in 1947: "the League was his life and … he was the life of the League", in a contemporary's words.[^1] [Una Marson](../People/PER-0013.md), who lodged with the Moodys at [164 Queen's Road](../Places/PLC-0039.md), Peckham, in autumn 1933 became the League's unpaid assistant secretary, and later editor of its journal.[^3][^4] [C. L. R. James](../People/PER-0014.md) served for a time on its executive and wrote for *The Keys*.[^1] Fryer gives the membership in 1936 as 262. Of these, 178 were in Cardiff, 34 in Liverpool, and 99 were white.[^1]

## The Keys

[*The Keys*](../Works/WRK-0010.md), the League's quarterly, was published from summer 1933. Its first issue included a picture of Marson, "already a leading member".[^5] Jarrett-Macauley records that its title came from the Ghanaian educator J. E. K. Aggrey's analogy of the black and white keys of a piano, "a sign of racial harmony". In 1934 it had a circulation of over two thousand worldwide.[^4] Later editors included the Barbadian Peter Blackman, in 1938–9, whose last editorial before the journal ceased publication on the eve of war attacked British ignorance of the empire.[^1]

## Campaigns and meetings

- **Racial discrimination.** The League took up the cases of Black seamen in Cardiff and the colour bar in employment and lodgings.[^1]
- **The Caribbean, 1937–8.** During the labour unrest in the Caribbean, Moody had three letters published in *The Times* in a fortnight. He presided at a protest meeting at the [Memorial Hall, Farringdon Street](../Places/PLC-0041.md), which demanded land redistribution, universal adult suffrage and "Federation of the West Indies with complete self-government".[^1] Fryer writes that League pressure helped bring about the Moyne Commission. The League, the Negro Welfare Association and the [International African Service Bureau](../Organisations/ORG-0009.md) submitted a joint memorandum to it.[^1]
- **Commissions in the forces.** After representations by Moody, the Colonial Office declared on 19 October 1939 that British colonial subjects "including those who are not of European descent" were eligible for emergency commissions. "We are thankful for this," Moody wrote, "but we are not satisfied."[^1]
- **The Charter for Coloured Peoples.** In July 1944 the League held a three-day conference in London to draw up a "Charter for Coloured Peoples". It demanded "full self-government" for colonial peoples and equal rights "whatever their colour", and in Fryer's view it foreshadowed the resolutions of the [Manchester Congress](../Events/EVT-0004.md).[^6]
- **Conferences.** Its twelfth annual general meeting was held in Liverpool in March 1943, attended by 39 delegates from 12 centres.[^6]

## Critics and allies

Younger radicals attacked Moody. In 1932 the *Negro Worker* called him a "typical 'Uncle Tom'", and he was criticised for not admitting Asians and for not backing WASU's boycott of the Colonial Office hostel.[^1] Fryer stresses the cooperation between the League and the radicals nonetheless. He quotes Ras Makonnen's verdict that the League "had quite a hold over the loyalties of the blacks who had settled in Britain".[^1]

## Fate

Moody died on 24 April 1947, ten days after returning from a tour of the West Indies and the United States. "The league he founded survived him by four years."[^6]
