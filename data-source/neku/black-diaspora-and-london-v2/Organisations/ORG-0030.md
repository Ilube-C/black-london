# Bogle-L'Ouverture Publications

*The Ealing-based Black publishing house founded by Jessica and Eric Huntley in 1968 or 1969, named after Paul Bogle and Toussaint L'Ouverture; it first published Walter Rodney's The Groundings with My Brothers, ran the Walter Rodney Bookshop and co-founded the International Book Fair of Radical Black and Third World Books.*

Bogle-L'Ouverture Publications was an independent Black publishing house in Ealing, west London. It was founded by the Guyanese activists [Jessica Huntley](../People/PER-0023.md) and her husband Eric Huntley. The British Library counts it, with New Beacon Books, among "Britain's pioneering independent Black publishers".[^1] Jonathan Oates calls it the third Black publisher in the United Kingdom, after New Beacon Books (1966) and Allison and Busby (1967).[^2]

## Founding and founders

The Huntleys had been members of the People's Progressive Party in British Guiana before coming to Britain in the late 1950s.[^1][^3] The publishing house grew out of their campaigning for the historian Walter Rodney. In a recorded interview of 1992, the Huntleys describe organising demonstrations after Jamaica banned Rodney in 1968, and printing some of his papers on a Gestetner duplicator. They also describe choosing the name, raising money in the community, and deciding the title and form of the first book. They mention the support of Barbara Joseph, Locksley Conway and the writer Andrew Salkey.[^4] In a talk of 1986, Eric Huntley explained that the name commemorated Paul Bogle and Toussaint L'Ouverture.[^5]

The founding date is given as 1968 by the British Library, Oates and London Remembers. London Remembers also gives 1969 in another sentence.[^1][^2][^3] The firm celebrated its tenth anniversary in 1979 and its fortieth year in 2008.[^6][^7]

## Aims

In his 1986 talk, Eric Huntley described a mission to challenge the stereotypes of Black people in published work. He noted the shortage of Caribbean literature written for readers other than a European audience.[^5] The catalogue's summary of the 1992 interview records the Huntleys discussing "the political nature of books published by Bogle-L'Ouverture" and "the importance of the written word and of children knowing their history". They also spoke of how few books by Black authors could be found in local libraries and schools.[^8]

## Publications

- **[*The Groundings with My Brothers*](../Works/WRK-0026.md)** by Walter Rodney was the firm's first book, according to Eric Huntley's 1986 talk. It was published in London in 1969.[^5][^1] Oates writes that work on it began in June and July 1969, with a first print run of 3,000 copies.[^2]
- **Walter Rodney's *How Europe Underdeveloped Africa*** followed "two years later", according to the same talk.[^5]
- **Poetry and fiction** were added to the list.[^5] Oates names *One Love* (1971), an anthology edited by Andrew Salkey, and Odette Thomas's children's book *Rain Falling, Rain Shining*.[^2] The archive catalogue notes the firm's titles *Dread Beat and Blood* and *Railton Blues*.[^9]
- **Lucy Safo's *Cry A Whisper***, published after the relaunch, won a Commonwealth prize for a first book in 1994, according to Oates.[^2]

## Premises

- **110 Windermere Road, South Ealing.** Oates writes that the first meetings were held at the Huntleys' home there.[^2]
- **[141 Coldershaw Road, West Ealing](../Places/PLC-0075.md).** The business moved to the front room of the Huntleys' house in 1972. Sales to the public began there in 1973, mostly by mail order. The council issued an enforcement notice because the house had no permission for business use, and a public inquiry was held at Ealing Town Hall in 1975.[^2] A plaque unveiled in October 2018 marks the firm's years there, 1972–75.[^2]
- **[5a Chignell Place, West Ealing](../Places/PLC-0076.md).** The bookshop opened in a former dress shop in October 1975, according to Oates.[^2] In 1980, the year of Rodney's assassination, it was renamed the Walter Rodney Bookshop.[^2] Minutes of meetings held at the shop in 1986 survive in the Huntley papers.[^10]

## Campaigns and alliances

The bookshop was repeatedly attacked by racists. Oates records broken windows, graffiti and threatening letters from 1977. Racist slogans were painted on the shop in October 1979, and the National Action Party claimed responsibility for an attack in January 1983. Eric Huntley said then that they would not be terrorised out of existence.[^2] London Remembers dates the attacks to 1977–79.[^3] According to the catalogue's summary of the 1992 interview, the Huntleys spoke of the National Front attacks on the bookshop and "the complicity of the police". They also described Book Shop Joint Action, an alliance of bookshops that the catalogue describes as "a response to fascist attacks".[^8][^11] Oates writes that ten other London bookshops signed a joint letter asking the Home Secretary for a meeting.[^2]

With New Beacon Books and Race Today, Bogle-L'Ouverture organised the annual [International Book Fair of Radical Black and Third World Books](../Events/EVT-0022.md), first held in 1982. Jessica Huntley was a joint director.[^5][^11][^12] Their papers also hold material on the [Black People's Day of Action](../Events/EVT-0023.md) of 2 March 1981.[^13]

## Links with people in the atlas

- **[Jessica Huntley](../People/PER-0023.md)** co-founded the firm and ran it with Eric Huntley.[^1][^3]

## Fate

Oates writes that the business closed in March 1991. It was relaunched at Shepherd's Bush Library in July 1993 with new editorial consultants.[^2] A reception for its fortieth year was held at Ealing Town Hall in 2008.[^7] The Huntleys' papers, including the firm's records, were deposited with the London Metropolitan Archives, now The London Archives, where they are catalogued as LMA/4463.[^14]

## Evidence notes

- **Founding year.** The British Library and Oates give 1968.[^1][^2] London Remembers gives both 1968 and 1969.[^3] The firm's own anniversaries point both ways: a tenth anniversary in 1979, a fortieth year in 2008.[^6][^7]
- **The bookshop's opening.** Eric Huntley's talk of 1986 refers to "founding the bookshop in 1972".[^5] In the 1992 interview the Huntleys date the bookshop to 1975, "initially at their home in Ealing".[^11] Oates dates the move to Coldershaw Road to 1972 and the opening of the Chignell Place shop to October 1975.[^2]
- **Number of attacks.** Oates gives seven recorded attacks between 1977 and 1981, but also calls the attack of January 1983 the eleventh.[^2]
- **Interviews.** The interview and talk are known here from the archive catalogue's summaries; the recordings were not heard.
