# Anti-Slavery Society

*The London society founded in 1823 to campaign for the abolition of colonial slavery, known as the Anti-Slavery Society; Thomas Pringle was its secretary from 1827, and Mary Prince sought its help at its office at 18 Aldermanbury in 1828.*

The Anti-Slavery Society was the main London organisation campaigning for the abolition of slavery in Britain's colonies in the decade before the Abolition Act of 1833. Its office was at [18 Aldermanbury](../Places/PLC-0023.md) in the City of London.[^1] It matters to this atlas because [Mary Prince](../People/PER-0008.md) went there for help in 1828. Its secretary, Thomas Pringle, published her *History* in 1831.[^2][^3]

## Founding and name

Vigne describes how, after the abolition of the slave trade in 1807, its veteran campaigners set up the African Institution to work against slavery itself. In 1823 a new body was established, "its aims and objects revealed by its name": the London Society for the Mitigation and Gradual Abolition of Slavery throughout the British Dominions.[^1] By 1827, Vigne writes, it was "already known simply as the Anti-Slavery Society", as more and more of its supporters demanded total and immediate abolition.[^1] Sara Salih likewise dates the Society's founding to 1823.[^4]

## Leaders and officers

Salih names William Wilberforce, Thomas Clarkson, Zachary Macaulay and James Stephen the elder as its leading members.[^4] Vigne lists the "officers" whom Pringle met on joining in 1827. They were Thomas Fowell Buxton, Wilberforce's successor as leader of the abolition movement, Henry Brougham, Thomas Clarkson, Stephen Lushington, Zachary Macaulay and James Stephen.[^5]

The day-to-day work was done by paid staff:

- **Thomas Pringle**, the Scottish poet, was recruited as secretary in May 1827.[^5] Vigne writes that Pringle as "the paid servant of the Society is rarely mentioned either in its records or in later histories".[^5]
- **The clerk.** Salih identifies Joseph Phillips as the Society's clerk.[^4] Vigne quotes the committee's minute of 1834, which thanks "the Secretary and Clerk [Thomas Hart]".[^6]
- **George Stephen**, son of James Stephen, was the Society's honorary solicitor.[^6] Lambert writes that he acted without fee "as he usually did with matters relating to their campaign".[^7]

## Aims

The Society's original name set out its aim: the mitigation and gradual abolition of slavery. Vigne describes how in the early 1830s the "immediatists", who wanted total and immediate abolition, challenged and overtook the "gradualists".[^1][^8] Its campaigning was aimed above all at the West Indies, with some attention to the Cape and Mauritius.[^5][^9]

## Publications

The Society published the *Anti-Slavery Monthly Reporter*. Vigne writes that Macaulay and Pringle were its joint editors from 1828, its fifth year. "Monthly" was later dropped from its title. From 1832 Pringle also edited the cheap *Anti-Slavery Record*.[^5] The title page of the third edition of *The History of Mary Prince* (1831) states that it was "supplied at trade price to Anti-Slavery Associations by Joseph Phillips, 18, Aldermanbury".[^3]

Pringle made clear, however, that the *History* was not a Society publication. In his preface he wrote that "the Anti-Slavery Society have no concern whatever with this publication, nor are they in any degree responsible for the statements it contains. I have published the tract, not as their Secretary, but in my private capacity".[^10]

## Mary Prince and the Society's office

In his Supplement to the *History*, Pringle writes that "About the latter end of November 1828" Prince found her way to the Society's office in Aldermanbury. Someone who knew her situation had advised her to go there for help. After a preliminary examination of her account, Pringle took her to George Stephen and asked him to investigate whether her freedom could be legally established if she returned to Antigua.[^2] In her own narrative Prince recalls a later visit. When her savings ran out she "was forced to go back to the Anti-Slavery office to ask a supply, till I could get another situation". She adds: "They were very good to give me a supply, but I felt shame at being obliged to apply for relief whilst I had strength to work." She then went into the service of "Mr and Mrs Pringle".[^11]

The *History* led to two libel actions in 1833, [Pringle v. Cadell](../Events/EVT-0010.md) and [Wood v. Pringle](../Events/EVT-0011.md). Lambert describes how the pro-slavery writer James MacQueen attacked the *History* and blamed the Demerara rising of 1823 on "the actions and propaganda of the Anti-Slavery Society".[^12][^13]

## Campaigns

- **The Agency Committee.** In the early 1830s Joseph Sturge and other Quakers, with George Stephen's support, formed the Agency Committee. It shared 18 Aldermanbury with the Society, ran meetings across the country, employed eighteen lecturers and organised petitions.[^8] Salih describes it as a breakaway by radical members of the Society.[^4]
- **The final campaign.** On 18 April 1833, Vigne records, 330 delegates met at Exeter Hall in the Strand and agreed an address to the government. The next day they presented it to the Prime Minister, Earl Grey. On 14 May Buxton brought into the Commons a petition signed by 187,000 women.[^14] The Abolition Act received the Royal Assent on 28 August 1833, and emancipation took effect on 1 August 1834.[^14]

## Fate

In November 1833 Pringle expected the Society's "dissolution" soon. Vigne writes that its final meeting did not take place until 5 August 1834, when Pringle's salary was halved.[^6] Pringle died in London on 5 December 1834.[^15]

## Evidence notes

- **The clerk.** Salih names Joseph Phillips as the Society's clerk, while the committee minute quoted by Vigne names Thomas Hart. Phillips appears on the *History*'s title page as the supplier to anti-slavery associations at 18 Aldermanbury.[^3][^4][^6] The sources consulted do not show whether the two men held the post at different times.
- **Pringle's title.** Pringle described himself as the Society's secretary, and Vigne and Salih call him so.[^10][^1][^16] Lambert's chapter calls him a member.[^7]
- **The Society's records.** Its minutes and the *Reporter* are known here through Vigne and were not consulted directly.
