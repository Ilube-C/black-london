## At a glance
Ladipo Solanke (c. 1884 – 1958) was a Nigerian Yoruba barrister and anti-colonial organiser who founded the [Nigerian Progress Union](../Organisations/ORG-0007.md) (1924) with Amy Ashwood Garvey and the [West African Students' Union](../Organisations/ORG-0018.md) (WASU, 1925) in London.[^1] Adi's *West Africans in Britain* gives his dates as 1886–1958.[^2] As WASU's first secretary-general and the first warden of its hostel, Africa House, he made the union the main social, cultural and political focus for West Africans in Britain for some 25 years.[^1] WASU's hostels are placed on [Camden Road](../Places/PLC-0035.md), where Africa House opened in 1933,[^3] and at [South Villas](../Places/PLC-0036.md) in Camden.[^2]

## Life and significance
Born in Abeokuta in southern Nigeria, Ladipo Felix Solanke studied law at Fourah Bay College in Sierra Leone and travelled to England in 1922 to complete his legal studies.[^1] He studied law at [University College London](../Organisations/ORG-0016.md) from 1923 to 1927 and qualified as a barrister.[^1][^2] After campaigning against racist articles in the British press, Solanke founded the Nigerian Progress Union with the assistance of [Amy Ashwood Garvey](../People/PER-0004.md) in July 1924.[^1] Adi describes it as founded by thirteen Nigerian students in London, largely through Ashwood Garvey's enthusiasm.[^2]

In 1925 Solanke and Dr Herbert Bankole-Bright founded the West African Students' Union (WASU), with Solanke as its first secretary-general.[^1] Adi traces it to a meeting of West African students in London in [August 1925](../Events/EVT-0016.md), at which Bankole-Bright urged them to unite.[^2] Many of his essays appeared in the union's journal *[Wāsù](../Works/WRK-0009.md)*, launched in March 1926,[^1][^3][^2] and for a time he had editorial responsibility for it, until he was relieved of that work.[^2] Between 1929 and 1932 he made a fundraising tour of West Africa that provided some of the money for a hostel in London.[^1]

When Africa House, the WASU hostel, opened in Camden in 1933, Solanke became its first warden; the National Archives places this first hostel on Camden Road, NW1.[^1][^3] Solanke and Olu Obisanya, later his wife, prepared the hostel, which officially opened on 9 March 1933.[^2] Fryer dates the opening of the Camden Road house to January 1933.[^4] The union's journal for January 1933 gives the hostel's address as 62 Camden Road, London NW1.[^5] [Constance Cummings-John](../People/PER-0015.md) was involved in WASU while training in London.[^1] In 1938, once its appeal had raised over £1,500, WASU bought freehold premises in Camden Square,[^2] opening a second hostel there;[^3] Fryer describes it as a move to new headquarters.[^4] Adi also refers to a WASU hostel at South Villas in Camden.[^2] Solanke and his wife ran the WASU hostel until the late 1940s. In 1944 he began a second fundraising tour of West Africa, during which he was made a chief; when he returned four years later he found WASU more radical, was replaced as warden of the new hostel, and was marginalised in the organisation he had founded.[^1] Adi records that he remained warden of Africa House; when WASU decided at the end of 1952 to close its older Camden hostel, Solanke and his supporters kept it going as 'WASU Un-incorporated' from January 1953, using his own money, and he spent his last years running it.[^2] He died of cancer in London in 1958.[^1][^2]

## Evidence notes
1. **Year of birth:** Adi and Sherwood give c. 1884;[^1] Adi's *West Africans in Britain* gives 1886.[^2] Both years are given here, and no exact date of birth is recorded.
2. **The founding of WASU:** Adi dates the founding meeting to August 1925 but gives neither the day nor the place;[^2] the National Archives says that the union operated from August 1925.[^3]
3. **'Africa House':** Adi and Sherwood give the name to the hostel opened in Camden in 1933.[^1] Adi (1998) uses it for the WASU hostel at the time of the Aggrey House dispute, and in the 1950s for the Camden hostel that Solanke kept running.[^2] Neither says whether the name passed to the premises opened in Camden Square in 1938. The house number of the Camden Road hostel, 62, comes from the union's own journal of January 1933;[^5] no number has been found for the South Villas hostel.
