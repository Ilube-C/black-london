## At a glance
Jomo Kenyatta (c. 1897 – 22 August 1978) was a Kenyan anti-colonial statesman, anthropologist, and President of independent Kenya.[^1] He lived for years at [95 Cambridge Street](../Places/PLC-0057.md) in Pimlico,[^2] was among those who formed the [International African Friends of Abyssinia](../Organisations/ORG-0008.md) in 1935,[^3] and studied social anthropology at the [London School of Economics](../Organisations/ORG-0023.md).[^4]

## Life and significance
Born in Kenya, Kenyatta, by then general secretary of the Kikuyu Central Association, sailed from Mombasa in February 1929 on his first trip to Britain,[^4] going on behalf of the Association to present Kikuyu grievances to the Colonial Office.[^5] He returned in 1930, staying briefly at 95 Cambridge Street in Pimlico, where he lived again from 1933 to 1937.[^2] His main source of income after his return from Russia was work as a linguistic informant at [University College London](../Organisations/ORG-0016.md) and SOAS, including work for Lilias Armstrong of UCL on Kikuyu phonology.[^4] Murray-Brown says London University's phoneticians first used him as a Kikuyu speaker in the winter of 1929, which gave him a small income for several years, and that University College had him on its books for an English course from January to July 1935 and a phonetics course from October 1935 to June 1936.[^1] In the autumn of 1934 he was one of some 250 Black extras in Alexander Korda's *[Sanders of the River](../Works/WRK-0021.md)*, which starred Paul Robeson and was filmed at Shepperton Studios.[^1]

Kenyatta met Bronisław Malinowski in December 1934. By the spring of 1935 he was Malinowski's personal student, and that autumn he enrolled at the London School of Economics for the three-year postgraduate diploma in social anthropology, attending Malinowski's seminar.[^4][^5] He submitted his diploma thesis at the end of 1937; it became *[Facing Mount Kenya](../Works/WRK-0020.md)*, published in 1938 with an introduction by Malinowski.[^4][^6] In 1937, when the International African Friends of Abyssinia became the [International African Service Bureau](../Organisations/ORG-0009.md), he and Amy Ashwood Garvey were named its vice-presidents; Berman names Padmore, James and Makonnen as his colleagues there.[^3][^4] Murray-Brown says he became an honorary secretary of the International African Friends of Abyssinia, the body James had formed.[^1] He attended the 1945 Fifth Pan-African Congress in [Manchester](../Places/PLC-0014.md).[^1][^7]

## Connections

- **[Ladipo Solanke](../People/PER-0011.md)** — Jomo Kenyatta met Ladipo Solanke on arriving in London in 1929, when Solanke's West African Students' Union gave him hospitality; Adi describes them as friends.[^8]
- **[George Padmore](../People/PER-0005.md)** — George Padmore and Jomo Kenyatta worked together from 1930, first in Padmore's Comintern network in Germany and Moscow, then in London's Pan-African organisations of the 1930s.[^9]
- **[Paul Robeson](../People/PER-0019.md)** — Jomo Kenyatta and Paul Robeson met during the filming of Sanders of the River in 1934–35 and became better acquainted, visiting each other's flats.[^10]
- **[C. L. R. James](../People/PER-0014.md)** — C. L. R. James and Jomo Kenyatta worked together in the International African Friends of Abyssinia; James introduced Kenyatta to his publisher and reviewed Facing Mount Kenya in 1938.[^11]
- **[95 Cambridge Street](../Places/PLC-0057.md)** — Jomo Kenyatta lodged at 95 Cambridge Street, Pimlico, briefly in 1930 and again from 1933 to 1937.[^12]
- **[London School of Economics (LSE)](../Organisations/ORG-0023.md)** — Jomo Kenyatta studied social anthropology under Bronisław Malinowski at the London School of Economics from 1935, submitting his diploma thesis at the end of 1937.[^13]

## Evidence notes
1. **Year of birth:** English Heritage gives c. 1894,[^2] and a 1961 Institute of Current World Affairs report says "around 1895".[^5] Murray-Brown sets out the evidence: Kenyatta's own account to the Carter Commission in 1932 would place his birth near 1890, while a later account points to c. 1897–8, which Murray-Brown thinks nearer the truth.[^1] Angelo gives around 1895.[^14] No exact date of birth is recorded here.
2. **1930–31:** The same 1961 report says that he returned to Kenya in September 1930 and came back to England in May 1931, staying for the next fifteen years.[^5]
