## At a glance
Beryl Agatha Gilroy (30 August 1924 – 4 April 2001)[^1] was a Guyanese novelist and educator who became Camden's first Black headteacher at Beckford Primary School in [West Hampstead](../Places/PLC-0069.md) in 1969;[^2] the *Guardian* obituary calls her London's first Black head teacher.[^1] She wrote the autobiographical *[Black Teacher](../Works/WRK-0023.md)* (1976).[^1]

## Life and significance
Born in Springlands, Berbice, in British Guiana, Gilroy attended the teachers' training college in Georgetown from 1943 to 1945, leaving with a first-class diploma, and came to England for further study, arriving in 1951.[^1] The timeline in the 2021 edition of *Black Teacher* shows her lecturing in British Guiana until 1951 and taking an Advanced Diploma in Child Development at the University of London in 1952–3.[^3] After first facing problems in finding work as a teacher, she taught in Inner London Education Authority schools from 1953 to 1956, the period recounted in *Black Teacher*.[^1] Before she found a teaching job she worked as a clerk in a shop and as a maid.[^4] In *Black Teacher* she describes waiting months for a letter from the education authority's Divisional Office placing her in a school, working meanwhile in a mail-order sweatshop in the East End, and being turned down for a clerical job with a trade union because of her colour.[^3] The timeline lists her first posts as St Joseph in the Fields, Bethnal Green (1954), Bell Street School, Lisson Grove (1955), and the London County Council (1956).[^3]

From 1959 to 1968 she brought up her family while writing textbooks and children's books; in 1968 she rejoined the ILEA, becoming deputy head and then head of Beckford Primary School.[^1] The Greater London Authority dates her headship to 1969.[^2] Her direct work in schools ended in 1982.[^1] In 1976 she published *Black Teacher*.[^1]

In 1982 she joined the University of London [Institute of Education](../Places/PLC-0053.md) and the ILEA's Centre for Multicultural Education, applying her knowledge of psychology to her teaching experience; she later took a doctorate in counselling psychology, and was a founder member of Camden Black Sisters.[^1] Her first novel, *[Frangipani House](../Works/WRK-0024.md)* (1986), set in Guyana, explored ageing.[^1] Busby, writing in 1992, describes her as having completed a PhD in counselling psychology in the 1980s and become a child psychotherapist and multicultural researcher at the Institute; *Frangipani House* won the GLC Black Literature Prize and was followed by *Boy-Sandwich* (1989). Busby also calls her the first Black headmistress in her North London borough.[^4] The 2021 timeline gives an MA in Education from Sussex (1980), work as a counsellor and psychotherapist at the Tavistock Clinic (1980–3), research at the Centre for Multicultural Education at the Institute of Education (1983–9) and a PhD in counselling psychology from Century University (1983–7). It also records her deputy headship at Montem Primary School (1968–9) before her Beckford headship (1969–82), and her death at the Royal Free Hospital on 4 April 2001.[^3]

## Evidence notes
1. **First Black headteacher:** The Greater London Authority calls her Camden's first Black headteacher and one of London's first;[^2] the *Guardian* obituary calls her London's first Black head teacher,[^1] and Busby the first Black headmistress in her north London borough.[^4] Each description is attributed to its source.
2. **Her arrival and her diploma:** The *Guardian* obituary gives 1951 for her arrival,[^1] and the 2021 timeline shows her lecturing in British Guiana until 1951; the timeline places her Advanced Diploma in Child Development at the University of London in 1952–3 without naming a college.[^3]
