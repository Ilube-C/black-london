## At a glance
Kwame Nkrumah (21 September 1909 – 27 April 1972) was a Gold Coast revolutionary, Pan-African theorist, and Prime Minister of the Gold Coast from 1952[^1][^2] and the first Prime Minister of independent Ghana from 1957, then its President from 1960 until a military coup deposed him in 1966.[^3] In London from 1945 to 1947, he worked with [George Padmore](../People/PER-0005.md) to organise [the October 1945 Fifth Pan-African Congress](../Events/EVT-0004.md) and was a founder and general secretary of the [West African National Secretariat](../Organisations/ORG-0024.md).[^1] He lived at [60 Burghley Road](../Places/PLC-0054.md) in Kentish Town.[^4] The Secretariat's office was a small room at [94 Gray's Inn Road](../Places/PLC-0056.md).[^5] Nkrumah says that he and Padmore worked in Padmore's flat;[^5] Padmore lived at [22 Cranleigh Street](../Places/PLC-0012.md) from 1941 to 1957.[^6]

## Life and significance
Born in Nkroful, in the Nzima region of the Gold Coast, around 1909, Francis Kwame Nkrumah went to London in 1945, ostensibly to study at London University.[^1] His own account dates his birth to September 1909, and he left New York for London in May 1945.[^5] George Padmore met him at Euston Station and took him first to the [West African Students' Union](../Organisations/ORG-0018.md) hostel; finding it too formal, he soon found a room at 60 Burghley Road, his home from June 1945 until November 1947.[^5] Sherwood adds that a room at the hostel had been reserved for him in advance, that he moved from there to Ako Adjei's lodgings at 25 Laurier Road, Tufnell Park, and that Adjei helped him find the Burghley Road room, which she places in Tufnell Park.[^7]

On arriving he enrolled at [Gray's Inn](../Organisations/ORG-0004.md) and arranged to attend lectures at the [London School of Economics](../Organisations/ORG-0023.md), where he heard Harold Laski; later he also registered at [University College](../Organisations/ORG-0016.md) to read philosophy.[^5] He became an active member of the West African Students' Union and was soon elected its vice-president; he met and worked with Padmore and threw himself into organising the Fifth Pan-African Congress, held in [Manchester](../Places/PLC-0014.md) in October 1945.[^1] He and Padmore became joint secretaries of the Congress's Organisation Committee and worked night and day in Padmore's flat.[^5]

After the Manchester Congress, Nkrumah was one of the founders and the general secretary of the West African National Secretariat.[^1] He accepted its secretaryship, and its office was a small room at 94 Gray's Inn Road,[^5] space that Sherwood says came from the Gold Coast lawyer K. O. Larbi.[^7] He started and edited a monthly paper, *The [New African](../Works/WRK-0018.md)*, which became the Secretariat's voice in London until lack of funds stopped it;[^5] Adi and Sherwood call the paper *New Africa*. During his time in Britain he also published *[Towards Colonial Freedom](../Works/WRK-0019.md)*.[^1] On 14 November 1947 he left London for Liverpool and home.[^5] In November 1947 he returned to the Gold Coast.[^4] He died of cancer in Bucharest on 27 April 1972, in the sixth year of his exile.[^3]

## Evidence notes
1. **Date of birth:** His 1957 autobiography says that his birth was not registered by date; the priest who baptised him recorded 21 September 1909, the date he used on official documents, but he himself thought Saturday 18 September 1909 more likely.[^5] Adi and Sherwood give c. 1909.[^1]
2. **Prime Minister, 1951–52:** Released from prison in 1951 to become Leader of Government Business, he was formally made Prime Minister in 1952.[^1][^2]
3. **60 Burghley Road:** English Heritage places the house in Kentish Town;[^4] Sherwood places it in Tufnell Park.[^7]
4. **The WASU hostel in 1945:** Nkrumah and Sherwood say that he stayed first at the West African Students' Union hostel but do not give its address.[^5][^7]
