# Fifth Pan-African Congress (Manchester)

*The Pan-African Congress held at Chorlton Town Hall, Manchester, in October 1945, organised by the Pan-African Federation, with W. E. B. Du Bois as International President; its declarations demanded colonial independence.*

The Fifth Pan-African Congress met in [Chorlton Town Hall](../Places/PLC-0014.md), Manchester, in October 1945. The sessions recorded in George Padmore's published report run from 15 to 19 October.[^1] Organised by the Pan-African Federation, it gathered delegates from African and Caribbean political parties, trade unions and student bodies, as well as Black organisations in Britain.[^1][^2] Its declarations called for an end to colonial rule. Peter Fryer, quoting the historian Imanuel Geiss, describes it as "a landmark … in the history … of decolonization".[^2]

## Purpose and origins

Fryer places the Congress in "a ferment of pan-African activity" during the Second World War.[^2] Towards the end of 1944 the [International African Service Bureau](../Organisations/ORG-0009.md) had joined other Black organisations in Britain to form the Pan-African Federation, a "loose umbrella association". Its aims included "self-determination and independence of African peoples" and "the total abolition of all forms of racial discrimination".[^2] Padmore's report records that he proposed the Congress as chairman of the Bureau and that the Federation drew up the agenda. A delegate conference in Manchester on 11–12 August 1945 reviewed the preparations.[^1]

According to the participant Ras Makonnen, quoted by Fryer, Manchester was chosen for three reasons. Makonnen had businesses and political contacts there. The city had become "quite a point of contact with the coloured proletariat in Britain". And there were "age-old connections between cotton, slavery and the building up of cities in England".[^2] The Congress was timed to follow the World Federation of Trade Unions conference in Paris, and an Anti-Colonial Peoples' Conference held in London on 10 June 1945 preceded it.[^2]

## Organisers

The Pan-African Federation, with Dr Peter Milliard as president and T. R. Makonnen as treasurer, organised the Congress.[^1] [Kwame Nkrumah](../People/PER-0016.md) arrived in London in 1945 and joined Padmore, Makonnen and Peter Abrahams in the preparations. He writes that "George Padmore and I became joint secretaries of the Organisation Committee", working from Padmore's flat.[^3] Adi and Sherwood also record that Padmore enlisted Nkrumah's help with the organisation.[^4] [W. E. B. Du Bois](../People/PER-0001.md), then 77, flew from New York to attend and was elected International President.[^1][^2] Thomas C. Holt writes that Du Bois joined Amy Jacques Garvey and Padmore in sponsoring the Congress.[^5]

## Who took part

The surviving accounts give different totals:

- **About 90 delegates and 11 fraternal delegates and observers,** according to Fryer, who also gives a regional breakdown.[^2]
- **"Some two hundred delegates holding mandates",** according to Peter Abrahams in Padmore's report.[^1]
- **"Over 200 delegates",** according to Nkrumah.[^3]
- **200 delegates,** according to the University of Manchester.[^6]

Five of the atlas's figures are documented in the Congress record, and a sixth through his organisation:

- **[W. E. B. Du Bois](../People/PER-0001.md)**, International President. He chaired the sessions of 16, 17 and 18 October recorded in the report, and presented the Congress's memorandum to the United Nations.[^1]
- **[Amy Ashwood Garvey](../People/PER-0004.md)** chaired the first session, on 15 October, which was devoted to "The Colour Problem in Britain".[^1]
- **[George Padmore](../People/PER-0005.md)** proposed the Congress, opened the session of 16 October and served as a rapporteur on the Caribbean. He later edited the published report.[^1]
- **[Kwame Nkrumah](../People/PER-0016.md)**, listed as "F. K. Nkrumah (Gold Coast)", was rapporteur for "Imperialism in North and West Africa" on 16 October.[^1]
- **[Jomo Kenyatta](../People/PER-0017.md)**, secretary of the Kikuyu Central Association, was rapporteur for "The East African Picture" on 17 October. He opened by saying that he had "to report on six territories".[^1]
- **The [West African Students' Union](../Organisations/ORG-0018.md)**, founded by [Ladipo Solanke](../People/PER-0011.md), was among the British organisations represented. Fryer notes that it "played an active part" in the Congress.[^7][^2]

Other delegates included I. T. A. Wallace-Johnson, Hastings Banda, Obafemi Awolowo and the South African writer Peter Abrahams, who served as publicity secretary.[^1][^2]

## Sessions and debates

Padmore's report sets out the programme by session:[^1]

| Date | Session | Chair | Rapporteurs |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 15 October | The Colour Problem in Britain | Amy Ashwood Garvey | E. J. Duplan, E. A. Aki Emi |
| 15 October | Official opening; welcome by the Lord Mayor of Manchester | not named | — |
| 16 October | Imperialism in North and West Africa | W. E. B. Du Bois | F. K. Nkrumah |
| 16 October | Oppression in South Africa | W. E. B. Du Bois | Peter Abrahams, Marko Hlubi |
| 17 October | The East African Picture | W. E. B. Du Bois | Jomo Kenyatta |
| 17 October | Ethiopia and the Black Republics | W. E. B. Du Bois | T. R. Makonnen |
| 18 October | The Problem in the Caribbean | W. E. B. Du Bois | George Padmore, Ken Hill |
| 19 October | Final working session | Peter Milliard | W. E. B. Du Bois |

Fryer notes that the whole first day was given to racism in Britain itself. Speakers described the poverty of Black seamen and workers, discrimination against British-born young people of colour in Cardiff, and police treatment of Black men in London's East End.[^2] On the last working day, Amy Ashwood Garvey raised the position of Black women: "Very much has been written and spoken of the Negro, but for some reason very little has been said about the black woman."[^1]

## Resolutions and declarations

The Congress adopted resolutions on each region, including West Africa, East Africa, South Africa and the High Commission Territories, and the West Indies. It also passed resolutions on Ethiopia, Liberia and Haiti, on "Coloured Seamen in Great Britain" and on the "Colour Bar Problem in Great Britain".[^1] Fryer records that the colour-bar resolution demanded that discrimination on grounds of race, creed or colour be made a criminal offence.[^2]

Its two summary declarations were "The Challenge to the Colonial Powers" and the "Declaration to the Colonial Workers, Farmers and Intellectuals". The second affirmed "the right of all Colonial peoples to control their own destiny" and closed "Colonial and Subject Peoples of the World—Unite!"[^1] Fryer states that Nkrumah drafted the second declaration.[^2] Adi and Sherwood write more cautiously that he "claimed to have drafted" a "Declaration to the Colonial Peoples of the World".[^4]

## Significance

Fryer notes that the British press "all but ignored" the Congress. He stresses what Padmore called its "plebeian character": trade unions and political parties sent delegates for the first time, where earlier congresses had centred on "a small intellectual élite".[^2] Several delegates later led independent states, Nkrumah and Kenyatta among them.[^2][^6] In London, Nkrumah went on to found the [West African National Secretariat](../Organisations/ORG-0024.md) with Wallace-Johnson; its office at [94 Gray's Inn Road](../Places/PLC-0056.md) was obtained from K. O. Larbi, who had attended the Congress.[^8]

## Evidence notes

- **Dates.** The sessions in Padmore's report run from 15 to 19 October 1945. Abrahams, writing in the same report, gives "October 13th–21st".[^1]
- **Attendance.** Totals range from about 90 delegates (Fryer) to over 200 (Abrahams, Nkrumah, the University of Manchester).
- **Chairing the opening.** Padmore's report puts Amy Ashwood Garvey in the chair on 15 October and Du Bois on 16 October.[^1] Holt writes that Du Bois "cochaired the opening session" with her.[^5] Nkrumah writes of the "joint chairmanship" of Du Bois and Milliard.[^3]
- **Authorship of the declaration.** The report names no drafter for either declaration.[^1]
- **Shared presence.** Being present at the same Congress does not by itself show that two delegates met. Du Bois himself wrote that the Congress was where he "first saw Nkrumah". Sherwood, however, writes that Du Bois had already met Nkrumah at a conference earlier in 1945 and forgotten it.[^9]
