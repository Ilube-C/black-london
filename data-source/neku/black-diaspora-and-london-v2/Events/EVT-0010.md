# Court of Common Pleas Trial (Pringle v. Cadell)

*Thomas Pringle's libel action against the London publisher Thomas Cadell, heard in the Court of Common Pleas on 21 February 1833, at which Mary Prince briefly gave evidence.*

| :--- | :--- |
| Date | 21 February 1833 |

*Pringle v. Cadell* was the first of two libel trials that followed the publication of [*The History of Mary Prince*](../Works/WRK-0006.md) in 1831. It was heard in the Court of Common Pleas on 21 February 1833.[^1] [Mary Prince](../People/PER-0008.md) was called as a witness, briefly.[^1]

## Why the case was brought

In November 1831 the pro-slavery writer James MacQueen attacked the *History* in *Blackwood's Edinburgh Magazine*. He called Prince's account a "pretended history" and Prince a "despicable tool". He also insinuated an improper relationship between Prince and the abolitionist Thomas Pringle, who had published her narrative.[^2] Pringle wrote that he was "prosecuting him for libel".[^2] Because MacQueen and the magazine's owner, William Blackwood, lived in Scotland, outside the English courts' jurisdiction, Pringle sued Blackwood's London publishing partner, Thomas Cadell, instead.[^2] George Stephen, like Pringle a member of the [Anti-Slavery Society](../Organisations/ORG-0013.md), served the writ and acted without fee.[^2]

## The hearing

The trial was due to begin on 19 December 1832 but was delayed to the Hilary term. As a result, Lambert notes, neither side could call witnesses from the Caribbean, who had had to leave Britain.[^3] On 21 February 1833 Serjeants Wilde and Jones argued that MacQueen's article, published in London by Cadell, had imputed "intentional falsehood and gross misconduct" to Pringle. Wilde read passages of it aloud.[^3] Prince "was briefly called as witness, but only to confirm that she had dictated her account for Pringle and she was not cross-examined".[^3] For Cadell, Serjeant Coleridge argued that his client was a "respectable" publisher who had known no more of the article's contents "than any one of the jury". He conceded that "one or two expressions" would have been better omitted.[^4]

## Outcome

The jury found for Pringle but awarded nominal damages of £5. Pringle had asked for £2,000. With costs, Cadell had to pay £160, and the article was to be amended to remove the passages about Pringle and the women of his household.[^4] Less than a week later, Pringle was himself defending a libel action brought by Prince's former enslaver (see [*Wood v. Pringle*](../Events/EVT-0011.md)).[^4]

## Evidence notes

- **Prince's part.** Prince's role in this trial was formal. Little of her life story was heard, and her full testimony came only in the second trial.[^3]
- **Location.** The sources consulted do not say where the Court of Common Pleas sat for this hearing.
- **Newspaper reports.** The account above follows Lambert's reading of reports in *The Times* and the *Morning Chronicle*, which were not themselves consulted.
