# Baptism of Gustavus Vassa

*The baptism of the boy then called Gustavus Vassa, later known as Olaudah Equiano, at St Margaret's Church, Westminster, on 9 February 1759, with Miss Guerin as godmother.*

| :--- | :--- |
| Date | 9 February 1759 |

On 9 February 1759 the boy then known as Gustavus Vassa, later [Olaudah Equiano](../People/PER-0006.md), was baptised at [St Margaret's Church, Westminster](../Places/PLC-0015.md). The parish register, as transcribed by Vincent Carretta, describes him as "Gustavus Vassa a Black born in Carolina 12 years old".[^1]

## How it came about

Equiano's own account in his *Interesting Narrative* (1789) explains the baptism. Back in London with his master, the naval officer Michael Henry Pascal, he was sent to wait on the Miss Guerins, who had been kind to him before and who sent him to school. Their servants told him that he "could not go to Heaven unless I was baptized", which made him "very uneasy". When he asked the eldest Miss Guerin, she pressed Pascal, who had earlier refused. Pascal, "being under some obligation to her brother", gave way:

> so I was baptized in St. Margaret's church, Westminster, in February 1759, by my present name. The clergyman, at the same time, gave me a book, called a Guide to the Indians, written by the Bishop of Sodor and Man. On this occasion Miss Guerin did me the honour to stand as godmother, and afterwards gave me a treat.[^2]

Carretta identifies the brother as Maynard Guerin and records that Miss Guerin and her brother stood as godparents. He identifies the book as Thomas Wilson's *An Essay towards an Instruction for the Indians* (1740).[^1] He suggests that Pascal may at first have refused for fear that baptism would weaken his claim to Equiano as property, given "the still widespread belief that Christians could not legally enslave fellow Christians". That is Carretta's interpretation, not something the register or the *Narrative* states.[^1]

## Significance

The baptism gave Equiano the name, Gustavus Vassa, under which he lived and wrote. Carretta argues that the clergyman's gift of a book gave him "access to literacy, an access he fully exploited".[^1] The register's "born in Carolina" is also one of the records behind the scholarly dispute over Equiano's birthplace, set out in his biography. Carretta questions the African birth that the *Narrative* describes, and Lovejoy defends it.[^1][^3]

## A neighbour in the same church

Carretta notes that [Ignatius Sancho](../People/PER-0007.md) had married at St Margaret's two months earlier and lived "only a few blocks away from the Guerins". He concludes that "Sancho and Equiano must have known each other at least by sight".[^1] This is Carretta's inference. No source consulted for this edition records that the two men met.
