# Second Pan-African Congress (London Session)

*The London sessions of the Second Pan-African Congress, held at Central Hall, Westminster, in late August 1921, before sessions in Brussels and Paris; convened by W. E. B. Du Bois, they adopted the outspoken "London Manifesto".*

The Second Pan-African Congress met in three cities in 1921: London, Brussels and Paris.[^1][^2] [W. E. B. Du Bois](../People/PER-0001.md) took the initiative in calling it, two years after the first Congress in Paris.[^1] The London sessions were held at Central Hall, Westminster, at the end of August.[^2][^3] They produced the most radical statement of the three. Fryer quotes the historian J. Ayodele Langley's verdict that the London portion was "perhaps the most radical" of the congresses of the 1920s.[^2]

## Purpose and organisation

The first Congress, organised by Du Bois, had met in Paris in February 1919 and petitioned the Peace Conference in moderate terms.[^2] For 1921 Du Bois arranged sessions in three capitals.[^1] Padmore's later history records that the London sessions were preceded by a conference with the International Department of the Labour Party. Beatrice Webb and Norman Leys were among those who attended.[^3] Fryer also names Leonard Woolf among the Labour figures consulted beforehand.[^2]

## Who took part

Padmore gives 113 delegates for the Congress as a whole: 41 from Africa, 35 from the United States, 24 from Europe and 7 from the West Indies.[^3] Fryer gives the same total for London.[^2] Fryer writes that three veterans of the [1900 Conference](../Events/EVT-0001.md) took the chair in turn: Du Bois, Dr John Alcindor, then president of the African Progress Union, and J. R. Archer. Archer introduced the Indian revolutionary Shapurji Saklatvala, who declared the solidarity of the Indian national movement with "the Coloured World".[^2][^4] Fryer also records that Samuel Coleridge-Taylor's widow and the American singer Roland Hayes were present.[^2]

Of the atlas's figures, only Du Bois is documented at the London sessions.

## Debates

Fryer reports that Alcindor opened with a plea for "restraint and circumspection". Other speakers attacked colonial rule directly.[^2] Albert Marryshow of Grenada condemned the power of the colony's governor and said that some Grenadian workers with families were paid as little as a halfpenny a day.[^2]

The disagreement came after London. At the Brussels sessions, Fryer writes, Blaise Diagne of Senegal called the London document's phrase on land ownership "Bolshevist" and refused to put it to the vote. Diagne had chaired the 1919 Congress. Instead he declared a milder compromise carried, over the protests of the English-speaking delegates.[^2] Padmore notes that the Paris sessions then adopted the London resolutions with minor corrections.[^3]

## The London Manifesto

The London sessions unanimously adopted a document later known as the Declaration to the World, or the London Manifesto.[^2] According to Fryer, it demanded "the recognition of civilised men as civilised despite their race and colour". It also upheld "the ancient common ownership of the Land and its natural fruits and defence against the unrestrained greed of invested capital".[^2] It charged that Britain had "systematically fostered ignorance among the Natives" and had declined "to grant to coloured Colonies those rights of self-government which it freely gives to white men".[^2] Padmore records the Paris version's principle that "the absolute equality of races, physical, political, and social, is the founding stone" of world peace.[^3]

## Significance

The 1921 sessions brought the Pan-African congresses back to London, where the 1900 Conference had met. The radical London resolutions survived the split at Brussels.[^2] A third congress met in London, at Denison House, Vauxhall Bridge Road, in November 1923.[^2] Padmore later described the congresses of the 1920s as having "centred around a small intellectual élite", in contrast with the [Manchester Congress of 1945](../Events/EVT-0004.md).[^5]

## Evidence notes

- **Dates.** Fryer gives the London sessions as 27 and 29 August 1921.[^2] Padmore's history gives "August 28th and 29th, 1921".[^3]
- **Chairs.** Padmore's account of the London sessions names no chair.[^3] The rotation of chairs above follows Fryer.
- **Manifesto text.** The text of the Manifesto was not consulted in full; the quotations come from Fryer and Padmore.
