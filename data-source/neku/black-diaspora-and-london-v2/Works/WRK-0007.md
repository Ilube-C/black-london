# *Hiawatha's Wedding Feast*

*Samuel Coleridge-Taylor's cantata setting part of Longfellow's The Song of Hiawatha, first performed at the Royal College of Music on 11 November 1898; it made him famous and became the first part of his Hiawatha trilogy.*

*Hiawatha's Wedding Feast* is a cantata for tenor solo, chorus and orchestra by [Samuel Coleridge-Taylor](../People/PER-0010.md), setting a section of Henry Wadsworth Longfellow's poem *The Song of Hiawatha*. It was first performed by the choir and orchestra of the [Royal College of Music](../Organisations/ORG-0015.md) on 11 November 1898, conducted by Sir Charles Stanford (see [the premiere](../Events/EVT-0015.md)).[^1] Coleridge-Taylor was twenty-three.

## Composition

His biographer W. C. Berwick Sayers writes that the work began as a college composition. It owed something to the "suggestions and criticisms" of Stanford, his composition teacher since 1892.[^2] Coleridge-Taylor told a friend that "the essential beauty of the poem" was "its naive simplicity".[^2] Novello agreed to publish it before the first performance.[^3]

## First performance and reception

On the night, Sayers writes, "the old hall of the Royal College" was crowded, with people sitting on the platform steps.[^3] The tenor solo "Onaway, Awake" "was recognised at once at its real worth".[^1] "Next morning he awoke to find himself indeed famous", Sayers writes, and every London paper reviewed the work.[^1]

## The trilogy

*The Wedding Feast* was planned as a complete work in itself. Sayers records that only while writing *The Death of Minnehaha* did Coleridge-Taylor decide to make the story into a trilogy, *The Song of Hiawatha*.[^4]

## Connections

Two years after the premiere, Coleridge-Taylor took charge of the music at the [First Pan-African Conference](../Events/EVT-0001.md) in London.[^5] He set African American melodies in his [*Twenty-Four Negro Melodies*](../Works/WRK-0008.md) (1905).[^6]
