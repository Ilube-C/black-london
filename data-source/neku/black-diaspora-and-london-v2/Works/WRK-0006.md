# *The History of Mary Prince, a West Indian Slave*

*Mary Prince's account of her life in slavery in Bermuda, Turks Island and Antigua and of her escape in London, dictated to Susanna Strickland and published by Thomas Pringle in 1831; the first narrative of a Black British woman's escape from slavery.*

*The History of Mary Prince, a West Indian Slave. Related by Herself* was published in London in 1831. Busby calls [Mary Prince](../People/PER-0008.md) the first Black British woman to escape from slavery and publish a record of her experiences. She notes that the *History* was published in London and Edinburgh and reached a third edition in its first year.[^1] Prince dictated it to Susanna Strickland, a friend of Thomas Pringle's who lodged with his family in Pentonville.[^2] Pringle, secretary of the [Anti-Slavery Society](../Organisations/ORG-0013.md), published it with a preface and a supplement.[^3]

## The narrative

Prince tells of her childhood in Bermuda, her sale away from her mother, and work in the salt ponds of Turks Island. She describes her years with John Wood in Antigua, and her journey to London with the Woods in 1828. In London she left them at [Leigh Street](../Places/PLC-0021.md).[^4] Near the end she speaks directly to English readers:

> All slaves want to be free — to be free is very sweet. I will say the truth to English people who may read this history that my good friend, Miss S—, is now writing down for me. I have been a slave myself — I know what slaves feel.[^5]

## Publication and controversy

The title page of the third edition shows that copies were "supplied at trade price to Anti-Slavery Associations by Joseph Phillips, 18, Aldermanbury".[^6] Pringle stated in his preface that the Anti-Slavery Society had "no concern whatever with this publication". He wrote that he had published it "not as their Secretary, but in my private capacity".[^3] The pro-slavery writer James MacQueen attacked it in *Blackwood's Edinburgh Magazine*. Two libel actions followed in 1833, [Pringle v. Cadell](../Events/EVT-0010.md) and [Wood v. Pringle](../Events/EVT-0011.md), at which Prince gave evidence.[^7]

## Evidence notes

- **Editing.** The *History* was taken down by Strickland and edited by Pringle. Lambert notes that at the 1833 trial Prince described parts of her life that she had told Strickland but that had been left out of the pamphlet, such as seven years spent with a Captain Abbot before her marriage. In his view her testimony shows how the *History* "had been shaped to appeal to its middle-class, British, antislavery audience".[^8]
- **The edition consulted.** Quotations follow Sara Salih's Penguin edition (2000), which reproduces the third edition of 1831.[^6]
