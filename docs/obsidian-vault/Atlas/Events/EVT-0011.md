*Wood v. Pringle* was heard in the Court of King's Bench on 27 February 1833.[^1] It was brought by [Mary Prince](Atlas/People/PER-0008.md)'s former enslaver, the Antigua slaveholder John Adams Wood, against Thomas Pringle, who had published [*The History of Mary Prince*](Atlas/Works/WRK-0006.md).[^1][^2] Unlike the [first trial](Atlas/Events/EVT-0010.md), this one put Prince's own testimony at the centre.

## The case against Pringle

Wood's counsel, Sir James Scarlett, presented his client as a slaveholder who wished for emancipation "as much as the Anti-Slavery Society", though not immediately.[^1] Wood alleged that Pringle had:

- published falsehoods about him and his wife;
- suggested that Wood had blocked Prince from employment in England so that she would have to return to Antigua;
- left out a letter that made allegations about Prince's conduct;
- misrepresented Wood's part in her marriage to Daniel James.[^1]

Scarlett called six witnesses, four of whom had known the Woods in Antigua, among them their married daughter. They described Prince as a well-treated servant. Under cross-examination, some were shown to be slaveholders themselves or to lack credibility.[^1][^3]

## Mary Prince's evidence

The Solicitor-General, Sir John Campbell, led for Pringle. He argued that the *History* had been published "solely for the purpose of opening the eyes of the public to the sufferings of the slaves by the personal narrative of one of the sufferers".[^3] "The first witness called was Prince herself."[^3] Her testimony was reported at length, though not verbatim, in *The Times* of 1 March 1833. She described the conditions and abuse she had suffered under the Woods, her failed attempts to persuade them to sell her, and her passage to England.[^3]

Under cross-examination she acknowledged a seven-year relationship with a Captain Abbot before her marriage, which had been left out of the *History*. Lambert argues that her testimony shows how the published narrative had been shaped for its British readers.[^3] Parts of her evidence drew laughter in court. Lambert, citing Kathryn Temple, reads that laughter as a sign of contemporary attitudes to Black women.[^4] Her answers also recorded her selling "coffee, yams, pigs" and taking in washing.[^4] Defence witnesses supported her account. Among them was Susan Brown, Pringle's sister-in-law, who said she had examined Prince's body and "found many marks of wounds upon her".[^4]

## Outcome

The verdict was split, with the jury finding for Wood on some counts and for Pringle on others. Lambert notes that the newspaper reports of the counts are "opaque and highly inconsistent".[^4][^2] Wood was awarded £25 in damages but not his costs.[^2]

## Evidence notes

- **Date of the report.** Salih's notes to the *History* date Prince's evidence to "the libel case which Wood brought against Pringle in March 1833" and cite *The Times* of 1 March 1833.[^5] Lambert dates the hearing to 27 February.[^1]
- **Transcripts.** No verbatim transcript of Prince's testimony was consulted.
