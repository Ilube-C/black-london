*International congress on relations between "so-called white and so-called coloured peoples", held at the University of London on 26–29 July 1911; W. E. B. Du Bois was a United States secretary and gave a paper on the Negro race in America.*

The First Universal Races Congress met at the University of London from 26 to 29 July 1911.[^1] It was not a Black political gathering. It was a large international meeting of scholars, officials and reformers, called to discuss "the general relations subsisting between the peoples of the West and those of the East, between so-called white and so-called coloured peoples".[^1] For the history of Black London it matters because a few Black delegates attended and used it to meet one another. Among them was [W. E. B. Du Bois](Atlas/People/PER-0001.md), and Fryer describes the first Black-run political journal in Britain as "in a sense a by-product" of the Congress.[^2]

## Purpose and organisers

The Congress's printed papers state its object in full. It was to discuss these relations "in the light of science and the modern conscience … with a view to encouraging between them a fuller understanding, the most friendly feelings, and a heartier co-operation".[^1] The invitation circular announced a congress "dealing with the general relations subsisting between West and East". English, French, German and Italian were its official languages.[^1] Its president was Lord Weardale, and its honorary organiser was G. Spiller, who edited the published papers.[^1] The list of vice-presidents was long and official; it began with the Prime Minister.[^1]

## Du Bois's part

Du Bois held a named office in the Congress. He was one of its honorary secretaries for the United States, at "20 Vesey Street, New York".[^1] His paper, "The Negro Race in the United States of America", was read in the sixth session. The editor printed it "in full" because of "its special value", although in smaller type because of its length.[^1] It opens with numbers rather than rhetoric:

> There were in 1900 in the United States and its dependencies 8,840,789 persons of acknowledged Negro descent … To-day the number is probably ten millions.[^1]

The paper then traces the slave trade, emancipation and the conditions of Black Americans. Adi and Sherwood record that Du Bois "returned to London in 1911" for the Congress, eleven years after the [1900 Pan-African Conference](Atlas/Events/EVT-0001.md).[^3]

## Other African and diaspora contributors

African contributors included J. Tengo Jabavu of King William's Town, who wrote on "Native Races of South Africa", and Dr Mojola Agbebi of Lagos, who wrote on "The West African Problem".[^1] Agbebi ended by hoping that "the principles for which the Congress stands will … go a long way towards the solution of the African problem".[^1] Adi and Sherwood note that the journalist Dusé Mohamed Ali ran the publicity and entertainments. They name Du Bois and the Sierra Leonean businessman John Eldred Taylor as "among the few Black men at the Congress".[^4]

## Outcomes

The Congress passed no programme of Black political demands. Fryer quotes Imanuel Geiss's judgement that it was "not a Pan-African event".[^2] Its lasting result for Black London came from the meetings it made possible. Taylor proposed financing a newspaper, and Dusé Mohamed Ali launched the *African Times and Orient Review* in July 1912 in partnership with him.[^4][^2] Fryer calls it "the first political journal produced by and for black people ever published in Britain".[^2]

## Evidence notes

- **Building.** The printed papers give the venue only as "the University of London"; the building is not named in the sources consulted.
- **Attendance.** The sources consulted do not establish whether any other figure in the atlas attended.
- **Discussion.** The volume prints the papers submitted rather than a record of discussion, so no debate between delegates is described here.

## Connected people

- **[W. E. B. Du Bois](Atlas/People/PER-0001.md)** — W. E. B. Du Bois was an honorary secretary of the First Universal Races Congress in London in July 1911 and contributed a paper to it.[^5]
