*The bombing of the African National Congress's London headquarters at 28 Penton Street, Islington, on the morning of Sunday 14 March 1982, by agents of the South African security services.*

| | |
| :--- | :--- |
| Date | 14 March 1982 |

<!-- MEDIA: image slot — photograph of the damaged building at 28 Penton Street, March 1982; to be selected and added by the editor -->

At 9 a.m. on Sunday 14 March 1982 a bomb exploded against the rear wall of [28 Penton Street](Atlas/Places/PLC-0068.md), Islington, the London headquarters of the exiled [African National Congress](Atlas/Organisations/ORG-0002.md).[^1] The Anti-Apartheid Legacy centre gives its weight as 11 lb. The Anti-Apartheid Movement's submission to the TRC gives 10 lb.[^1][^2] Nine former agents of the South African security apparatus later admitted to the Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC) that they had planned and carried out the attack.[^1]

## The attack

The ANC volunteer Vernet Mbatha was the only person in the building. He was slightly injured and escaped across the roofs of neighbouring buildings.[^1] The blast destroyed records and office equipment and blew off the back of the building. The ANC's printing press was thrown through a wall, but the ANC printer Patric Tariq Mellet recalled that it "was repairable and was soon set to work again".[^1] Debris was found nearly 400 metres away and windows were broken up to 500 metres away. The *Islington Gazette* wrote that the attack "revived wartime memories of the Blitz".[^1]

## Who was the target

The Anti-Apartheid Legacy centre suggests that [Oliver Tambo](Atlas/People/PER-0020.md), President of the ANC, "was expected to be in the building". He was due to address an anti-apartheid rally in Trafalgar Square later that day.[^1] At a TRC amnesty hearing in February 1999, Gill Marcus, who worked at 28 Penton Street, testified that she was usually in the office every Sunday morning, "except the day of the bomb". She also recalled meeting the security agent Craig Williamson there once.[^3]

## Responsibility and aftermath

The Anti-Apartheid Movement blamed Pretoria at once.[^1] At the TRC, the agents said that they had been led by Craig Williamson and had entered Britain on false papers with help from people at the South African Embassy in Trafalgar Square, where the bomb was assembled.[^1][^3] The Anti-Apartheid Movement's archives committee reported in its submission that Williamson had said the bomb was smuggled into Britain "through the South African diplomatic bag". He also said there was more than one team, for reconnaissance, surveillance and escape.[^2]

The British Council of Churches lent the ANC office space while the building was rebuilt. Archbishop Trevor Huddleston, President of the Anti-Apartheid Movement, launched an "ANC Office Appeal Fund", supported by Michael Foot, David Steel and Jack Jones.[^1] The ANC later returned to Penton Street.[^1]

## Evidence notes

- **The building today.** The present-day use of 28 Penton Street is not recorded here; see [ANC London Mission office](Atlas/Places/PLC-0068.md).
- **Weight and assembly.** The bomb's weight is given as 11 lb or 10 lb. The Anti-Apartheid Legacy centre says it was assembled at the embassy from parts brought into Britain; the Anti-Apartheid Movement reports Williamson's claim that it came through the diplomatic bag.
- **Tambo as the target.** That Tambo was the intended target is the Anti-Apartheid Legacy centre's inference ("It is likely that…"), not an established finding.
