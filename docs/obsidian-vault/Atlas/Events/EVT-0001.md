*Three-day conference at Westminster Town Hall, 23–25 July 1900, convened by Henry Sylvester Williams's African Association; it founded the Pan-African Association and adopted the "Address to the Nations of the World".*

The First Pan-African Conference met in London from Monday 23 to Wednesday 25 July 1900. Its plenary sessions were held at [Westminster Town Hall](Atlas/Places/PLC-0001.md).[^1][^2] It was the first gathering called to let people of African descent from Africa, the Caribbean, North America and Britain speak for themselves on the conditions of their peoples under European and American rule. Adi and Sherwood write that the organised Pan-African movement "can be said to have begun" with the founding of the [African Association](Atlas/Organisations/ORG-0001.md) in 1897 and the conference it convened three years later.[^3]

## Purpose and origins

[Henry Sylvester Williams](Atlas/People/PER-0003.md), the Trinidad-raised law student who served as the African Association's secretary, had conceived the idea of a world conference before the Association was formed.[^1] On 19 March 1898 an Association meeting agreed to issue a circular, signed by Williams and the Association's president, the Revd Henry Mason Joseph, announcing a conference two years ahead. Its stated purpose was "to take steps to influence public opinion on existing proceedings and conditions affecting the welfare of the Natives" in South Africa, West Africa and the British West Indies.[^1] After discussions with Black leaders who were in London on other business, Fryer writes, the scope was widened to "the treatment of native races under European and American rule", including the United States. Those named include Bishop James T. Holly, Bishop James Johnson and Booker T. Washington.[^1] Richards notes that the dates were chosen to fall just before the Paris Exhibition of 1900, so that visitors of African descent could attend both.[^4]

## Organisers

By April 1900 a Pan-African Conference committee had adopted the motto "Light and Liberty". Williams was its general secretary, Henry Mason Joseph its chairman, the Revd Thomas L. Johnson its vice-chairman, and the Trinidadian law student Richard E. Phipps its secretary for the West Indies.[^1] At a gathering of delegates at the Reform Club on 6 July, Williams said that the conference would be "the first occasion upon which black men would assemble in England to speak for themselves". Fryer reports that the British press first used the term "Pan-African" in its accounts of that meeting.[^1]

## Who took part

Fryer counts about 37 delegates and about ten other participants and observers. Richards gives the same figures, while Clarke writes of "the 40-odd delegates".[^1][^4][^2] Bishop Alexander Walters of the African Methodist Episcopal Zion Church took the chair. The vice-chairs were Frederick Johnson, a former attorney-general of Liberia, and the Haitian Benito Sylvain.[^1]

Three of the atlas's figures are documented as taking part:

- **[Henry Sylvester Williams](Atlas/People/PER-0003.md)** was the principal organiser.[^2]
- **[W. E. B. Du Bois](Atlas/People/PER-0001.md)** attended as a delegate and spoke on the second day.[^1] Adi and Sherwood record that he chaired the committee that drafted the conference's appeal to the nations.[^5]
- **[Samuel Coleridge-Taylor](Atlas/People/PER-0010.md)** "took charge of the musical side of the programme" and was afterwards elected to the executive of the new Pan-African Association.[^6] Richards calls him the youngest delegate.[^4]

The sources record all three in the same conference programme. That is evidence of shared participation, not of any private meeting between them.

## Proceedings

The accounts consulted describe papers followed by discussion, not formal debate between factions. Fryer's summary runs day by day:[^1]

- **23 July.** Bishop Walters opened with an address on "The Trials and Tribulations of the Coloured Race in America". The Bishop of London, Mandell Creighton, gave a speech of welcome that spoke of "the benefits of self-government". Papers were read by C. W. French of St Kitts, Anna H. Jones of Kansas and Anna J. Cooper of Washington. In the evening Benito Sylvain attacked colonial policy and the conduct of the colonising companies.
- **24 July.** Frederick Johnson opened a discussion on the progress of "our people". John E. Quinlan of St Lucia warned that British capital sought to re-enslave Black workers in South Africa. William Meyer, a Trinidadian medical student at Edinburgh, attacked pseudo-scientific racism, and Richard Phipps described discrimination in the Trinidad civil service. D. E. Tobias led a discussion, and Du Bois argued that denying Black people the chance to reach their potential hindered human progress as a whole.
- **25 July.** George James Christian of Dominica led a discussion on "Organized Plunder v. Human Progress". Bishop Walters closed by calling the conference "just the beginning of the work".

Clarke adds that Creighton gave a tea party for the delegates at Fulham Palace. She also quotes Du Bois's later account, in which the visit to the Houses of Parliament and tea on the Terrace were "the crowning honour of the series", with credit to "our genial secretary, Mr. H. Sylvester Williams".[^2]

## Resolutions and outcomes

The conference took three practical steps.

**A new organisation.** The African Association was merged into a new Pan-African Association.[^1][^6] Walters became president, the Revd Henry B. Brown vice-president, Williams general secretary and Dr R. J. Colenso treasurer. The executive included Coleridge-Taylor, Frederick J. Loudin, John Richard Archer, Henry Francis Downing, Anna J. Cooper and Jane Cobden Unwin.[^1] Its first stated aim was "to secure to Africans throughout the world true civil and political rights".[^1]

**An address to the world.** The conference unanimously adopted an address ["To the Nations of the World"](Atlas/Works/WRK-0001.md), to be sent to the heads of states where people of African descent lived.[^1] It was signed by Walters, Williams, Du Bois and Brown; the Du Bois Papers at the University of Massachusetts hold a signed copy with copies of the conference reports.[^7] The address opens:

> In the metropolis of the modern world, in this the closing year of the nineteenth century, there has been assembled a congress of men and women of African blood, to deliberate solemnly upon the present situation and outlook of the darker races of mankind. The problem of the twentieth century is the problem of the color line …[^8]

It called on Britain to give "as soon as practicable, the rights of responsible government to the black colonies of Africa and the West Indies".[^8]

**A petition to the Queen.** A petition was sent to Queen Victoria on forced labour, indenture and the pass system in South Africa and Rhodesia. The Colonial Secretary, Joseph Chamberlain, replied that the government would not "overlook the interests and welfare of the native races".[^1]

## Reception and aftermath

Fryer reports that the leading London newspapers covered the conference with little comment. The *Westminster Gazette* wrote that it marked "the initiation of a remarkable movement in history". W. T. Stead's *Review of Reviews* called the Address "a sign of the times of which we shall all do well to take note".[^1] Among those who gave money was the former MP Dadabhai Naoroji. Fryer interprets this as the earliest recorded instance of practical Afro-Asian solidarity in Britain.[^1]

After the conference Williams travelled to Jamaica, Trinidad and the United States to found branches. While he was abroad the treasurer, Colenso, dissolved the Association, citing lack of funds. Williams tried to revive it, and in October 1901 he launched a journal, *The Pan-African*, of which probably only one issue appeared.[^1]

## Significance

Du Bois later reused the address's phrase about "the problem of the colour-line" in *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903).[^1] For Adi and Sherwood, the conference and the Association that called it mark the beginning of the organised Pan-African movement.[^3] Fryer opens his account of the congresses of the 1920s by noting that, unlike the 1900 Conference, the first Pan-African Congress of 1919 met in Paris. London returned as a venue with the [1921 session](Atlas/Events/EVT-0003.md), and the movement's most influential gathering was the [Manchester Congress of 1945](Atlas/Events/EVT-0004.md).[^9]

## Evidence notes

- **Numbers:** delegate numbers differ slightly between accounts: about 37 delegates and ten others according to Fryer and Richards, "40-odd" according to Clarke.
- **Venue name:** the venue is named as Westminster Town Hall in Fryer and Clarke. Richards identifies the building as the one now known as Caxton Hall.
- **What the sources cover:** the consulted sources summarise the papers and discussions but record no vote or open disagreement within the conference. The account of each speaker's argument follows Fryer's summary. The conference's own published report was not consulted for this edition.
