*The National Chartist Convention, which assembled in London on 4 April 1848, and the mass meeting on Kennington Common on 10 April, whose processions William Cuffay's Demonstration Committee organised.*

In April 1848 the Chartists brought a national petition for the People's Charter to London. A National Convention of delegates assembled on 4 April, and on 10 April a mass meeting on Kennington Common was to escort the petition to Parliament.[^1] [William Cuffay](Atlas/People/PER-0009.md), a London tailor of West Indian descent, was a Convention delegate. He also chaired the "Demonstration Committee" that organised the procession bringing the petition to the Common and the one that was meant to carry it back across the Thames.[^1]

## The Convention

When the delegates assembled on 4 April, Cuffay moved the exclusion of the novelist George Reynolds, delegate for Derby, "on the grounds that he was not a Chartist".[^2] He accused the executive of shrinking "from its responsibility, now that things had come to a crisis". If Parliament rejected the petition, he said, "he should take the rejection as a declaration of war".[^2] Chase stresses that Cuffay was no "bellicose hothead". At sixty he was a man "of mild demeanour and quiet manners" whose militancy showed the new mood of London Chartism.[^2]

## Kennington Common, 10 April

The executive called off the return procession at the last moment, and Feargus O'Connor told the crowd to go home peaceably. According to an eyewitness quoted by Chase, Cuffay, who had not been told of the executive's plans, "spoke in strong language against the dispersal of the meeting". He called the Convention "a set of cowardly humbugs".[^1] When part of the crowd rushed the platform, the delegates standing on it, Cuffay among them, were thrown by the impact.[^1] Charles Kingsley's novel *Alton Locke* has Cuffay leap from the platform crying that the crowd had been "humbugged and betrayed". Chase notes that he probably did not leap, since his legs and spine were deformed from birth.[^2] *The Times* summed up his view as insisting "that the petition should have been accompanied by the people until opposed by the military".[^3]

## Aftermath

Cuffay returned to the Convention the next day despite his outburst.[^3] Soon afterwards, as he later explained, his wife Mary lost her work as a charwoman when an employer learnt that she was "the wife of Cuffay of the Convention".[^3] The Convention dissolved a fortnight later, and some London Chartists, Cuffay among them, turned to the conspiracy that ended at the [Orange Tree](Atlas/Events/EVT-0013.md) in August.[^4] Fryer names Cuffay as one of London's three delegates to the Convention and chairman of the committee that managed the procession.[^5]
