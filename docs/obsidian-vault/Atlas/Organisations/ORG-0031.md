*The British Black Panther Movement, founded in Notting Hill in 1968 by Obi Egbuna and led from the end of that year by Altheia Jones-LeCointe from Brixton; it took part in the Mangrove campaign and in 1973 renamed itself the Black Workers Movement.*

The Black Panther Movement (BPM) was a London Black Power organisation of the late 1960s and early 1970s. Its name came from the American Black Panther Party, which inspired it and with which it corresponded. Rosalind Wild stresses that the British Panthers were nonetheless "an independent organisation".[^1] It began in Notting Hill. Under its second leader, [Altheia Jones-LeCointe](Atlas/People/PER-0024.md), its centre moved to [Brixton](Atlas/Places/PLC-0006.md), and Bunce and Field describe how it became "a serious community-based organization".[^1][^2]

## Founding and founders

The Nigerian writer Obi Egbuna founded the movement. Wild dates its founding to April 1968, in Notting Hill.[^1] Bunce and Field write that early in 1968 Egbuna refused re-election as chair of the Universal Coloured People's Association, resigned, and "set up the Black Panthers". They call it "the first Panther group to be set up outside the United States".[^3] Its beginnings were small. Egbuna later wrote that "We began the Panthers with only three or four members".[^1]

Egbuna did not lead it for long. In July 1968 he was arrested over a pamphlet on what to do if police laid hands on a Black man at Speakers' Corner.[^1][^3] He was held on remand for five months, convicted on 10 December 1968 and given a suspended prison sentence. Its conditions kept him out of radical politics, and he withdrew from the movement at the end of 1968.[^4]

## Leaders and structure

After Egbuna's withdrawal, Wild writes, "Althea Lecointe, a Trinidadian postgraduate student, became the Panthers' new leader".[^4] This is [Altheia Jones-LeCointe](Atlas/People/PER-0024.md). Wild notes that the BPM was "the only Black Power organisation to have a female leader".[^5] She also notes that Jones-LeCointe kept herself anonymous outside the movement, so that little was written by or about her apart from her statements at the [Mangrove Nine trial](Atlas/Events/EVT-0025.md).[^6]

Membership was selective. Linton Kwesi Johnson, who joined as a Brixton teenager, recalled that "you couldn't become a Black Panther just like that". Recruits first joined the youth league and had to prove their commitment.[^4] Wild describes a "rigidly hierarchical structure" rising from the youth league through members and senior members to a central committee. The committee set policy and kept ideological and personal discipline.[^4] Members were expected to keep a strict moral code. Wild reports that the movement regarded sexism as "equivalent to racism".[^5] Bunce and Field call the leadership body the Central Core. Among its members was the writer Farrukh Dhondy.[^7]

The membership was mostly West Indian, but it also included Africans and several prominent Asian members. White people could not join or attend meetings, though the BPM accepted support from radical white organisations.[^8]

## Meeting places and branches

After Egbuna's departure, the BPM's organisational centre moved from Portobello Road in Notting Hill to [Shakespeare Road in Brixton](Atlas/Places/PLC-0079.md). Separate branches were started in Acton and Finsbury Park.[^4] The movement ran two Black People's Information Centres, at 38 Shakespeare Road, Brixton, and, until 1971, at 54 Wightman Road, Finsbury Park.[^9] In 1972 the novelist John Berger shared his Booker Prize money with the Panthers. Bunce and Field write that with it they bought a house at 37 Tollington Park, Finsbury Park, which was used among other things as a print shop.[^7]

## Aims and activities

The BPM's newsletter listed its activities under the heading "What we do in practice". They included door-to-door work in the community, weekly study of Black history, classes in political education, cultural activities, and attending the trials of Black defendants.[^5] Wild writes that it was "the most active" of the Black Power groups in promoting Black culture. It held plays, poetry readings and concerts that often drew several hundred people. Johnson ran cultural workshops for its youth league.[^9] Even so, it rejected cultural nationalism. Bunce and Field describe its "class analysis of racism", which let it reach out to white sympathisers and trade unionists.[^9][^2]

## Campaigns

- **The Mangrove march.** The [demonstration of 9 August 1970](Atlas/Events/EVT-0024.md) in Notting Hill was organised by the Action Committee for Defence of the Mangrove "in conjunction with the Black Panther Movement". Jones-LeCointe spoke before it set off.[^10]
- **The Mangrove Nine.** Jones-LeCointe was one of the nine defendants tried at the Old Bailey in 1971, and she conducted her own defence.[^6] During the trial, Bunce and Field write, the BPM "mobilized a sustained struggle in defence of the Panthers' leadership from its political base in South London".[^2]
- **The Oval House raid.** Police raided a Black Panther event at the Oval House in Kennington on 31 August 1970. Police notes recorded 400 people present.[^9] On 28 June 1971 Eddie Lecointe and two other Panthers were found guilty of riotous assembly arising from the fighting during the raid. All three received suspended sentences.[^2]

## Publications

The BPM published the *Black People's News Service*, later followed by *Black Life* and *Freedom News*.[^9] Bunce and Field write that *Freedom News* followed advice that [C. L. R. James](Atlas/People/PER-0014.md) gave the Panthers when he spoke to them: to write about members' own working lives rather than "Leninist rhetoric". Its editor was Eddie Lecointe, Jones-LeCointe's husband.[^7]

## Links with people in the atlas

- **[Altheia Jones-LeCointe](Atlas/People/PER-0024.md)** led the movement from the end of 1968.[^4][^11]
- **[Olive Morris](Atlas/People/PER-0025.md)** joined after her arrest in Brixton in 1969 ([the Gomwalk incident](Atlas/Events/EVT-0026.md)).[^12] The Lambeth Archives catalogue places her in the Youth Section of the movement's Brixton core and in the BPM Sisters Collective.[^13]
- **[C. L. R. James](Atlas/People/PER-0014.md)** addressed the Panthers, according to Bunce and Field, which does not make him a member.[^7]

## Splits, decline and fate

Wild writes that the Black Liberation Front was founded in early 1970 by members of the North and West London branches. They disagreed with "the increasingly strict Marxism-Leninism" of Jones-LeCointe's leadership and wanted a looser structure.[^14] In 1973 the movement renamed itself the Black Workers Movement. Wild writes that this was partly to make it more appealing to Asian workers, and cites an article on the change in the movement's own newspaper.[^15] A Lambeth Archives entry describes photographs from summer 1973 of "Black Workers Movement members (formerly the Black Panther Movement)" supporting a picket at Standard Telephones and Cables.[^16]

Bunce and Field describe an internal break-up. The leadership became "morally prescriptive", and disputes in the shared house at Tollington Park led Dhondy and Darcus Howe to withdraw. In their account, "the movement dissipated".[^7] *The Heart of the Race* refers simply to "the decline of the Black Panther movement", after which Morris helped set up Sabarr Bookshop.[^12]

## Evidence notes

- **Founding date.** Wild gives April 1968. Bunce and Field give "early in 1968" for Egbuna's break with the UCPA and his founding of the Panthers.[^1][^3]
- **Size.** Accounts differ. Tony Soares estimated twenty members in the Finsbury Park branch in 1970, and Johnson about fifty in the South London branch at its peak, though "we could mobilise hundreds". A 1968 police report estimated more than 800; Wild argues that it cannot have distinguished members from supporters.[^4][^8] Bunce and Field write of "hundreds of members and thousands of supporters". Howe, however, remembered that the Panthers "never exceeded 300 members", though the youth league was much larger.[^2][^7]
- **The end.** Wild records a renaming in 1973; Bunce and Field describe the movement breaking up. The sources consulted do not give a date when it ceased to exist.
- **Records.** Wild writes that the British Panthers "kept the fewest written records" of London's four main Black Power organisations.[^1]
