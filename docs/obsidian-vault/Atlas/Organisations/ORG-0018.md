*The West African Students' Union, founded in London in August 1925 with Ladipo Solanke as secretary-general; it ran the Africa House hostel, published the journal Wasu, lobbied the Colonial Office and became a training ground for West African nationalist leaders.*

The West African Students' Union (WASU) was founded at a meeting of West African students in London in August 1925 (see [Founding of WASU](Atlas/Events/EVT-0016.md)).[^1] For a quarter of a century, Fryer writes, it was "a social and political centre for its members". It voiced their criticism of colonial rule and of the discrimination they met in Britain, and "functioned as a training ground for leaders of the West African nationalist movement".[^2] The National Archives notes that it "operated from August 1925, and remained active until the 1960s".[^3]

## Founding and founders

The meeting that formed WASU followed a visit to London by Dr Herbert Bankole-Bright of Sierra Leone, who urged the students to unite. After "a heated debate" the proposal was carried unanimously.[^1] J. B. Danquah became the first president. [Ladipo Solanke](Atlas/People/PER-0011.md), who had founded the [Nigerian Progress Union](Atlas/Organisations/ORG-0007.md) the year before, became secretary-general, a post he held for twenty-five years.[^1] Fryer describes the founders as twenty-one law students who later became prominent in West Africa.[^2]

## Aims

WASU's eight aims included a hostel for students of African descent; a bureau of information and centre of research on Africa; "goodwill, better understanding and brotherhood"; "a true picture of African life and philosophy"; and "a spirit of national consciousness and racial pride".[^1] Its journal, [*Wasu*](Atlas/Works/WRK-0009.md) ("Preach"), was launched in March 1926 with the message of "co-operation" among West Africans.[^4]

## Leadership and members

Solanke was the Union's central figure as secretary-general and later warden of its hostel.[^1][^5] [Kwame Nkrumah](Atlas/People/PER-0016.md), who joined on arriving in London in 1945, writes that he "later became vice-president".[^6] [Paul Robeson](Atlas/People/PER-0019.md) became a patron and gave money.[^2] Adi notes that although Nigerians first dominated WASU, by 1926 its president and vice-president both came from the Gold Coast.[^7]

## Homes and meeting places

- **1928.** WASU moved into a house put at its disposal for a year by Marcus Garvey.[^2] A Special Branch report of June 1929 recorded that Solanke and [Jomo Kenyatta](Atlas/People/PER-0017.md) were then both living in Garvey's house.[^8]
- **After 1929.** When Garvey's lease expired, WASU sent Solanke to West Africa to raise funds. He returned from his three-year mission with about £1,500, having also founded branches called "fraternities".[^2]
- **1933.** WASU opened a hostel and centre in Camden Road, [Africa House](Atlas/Places/PLC-0035.md). Adi gives the official opening as 9 March 1933; Fryer says January 1933.[^5][^2] WASU refused Colonial Office conditions that would have placed the hostel under government control. When the Colonial Office opened its own hostel, Aggrey House, WASU formed an Africa House Defence Committee and organised a boycott.[^2][^9]
- **1938.** With over £1,500 raised by appeal, WASU bought freehold premises in Camden Square. A dispute followed over a trust deed that made the Union "merely tenants, at will, of the Trustees".[^10] Fryer calls the new headquarters "a hive of intellectual and political activity" and "a market-place of ideas".[^2] The atlas records a WASU hostel at [South Villas](Atlas/Places/PLC-0036.md) in Camden.

## Campaigns and decisions

Fryer writes that the Italian invasion of Ethiopia pushed WASU towards a more anti-imperialist position in the 1930s.[^2] During the Second World War a WASU Parliamentary Committee met twice a month with sympathetic MPs, and the Union's 1942 conference "demanded immediate self-government and independence within five years of the end of the war".[^2] Africa House received ministerial visitors. Later in 1940 the Colonial Secretary, Lord Lloyd, "made history" by visiting Africa House and was presented with seventeen points on greater democracy in West Africa. In 1941 the Deputy Prime Minister, Clement Attlee, was questioned by WASU and spoke in support of increasing self-government.[^11] WASU took part in the [Fifth Pan-African Congress](Atlas/Events/EVT-0004.md) in 1945.[^2]

## Fate

Solanke and his wife ran the WASU hostel until the late 1940s.[^12] The Union remained active until the 1960s.[^3] The consulted sources do not record when it formally ceased.

## Evidence notes

- **The 1933 opening.** Adi gives 9 March 1933 for the official opening of Africa House; Fryer says January 1933. The two dates may refer to different stages.
- **Meetings.** The records consulted do not establish how often the Union met or who attended particular meetings, beyond the events named here.

## Connected people

- **[Ladipo Solanke](Atlas/People/PER-0011.md)** — Ladipo Solanke founded the West African Students' Union in London in 1925 with Herbert Bankole-Bright and was its secretary-general for twenty-five years.[^13]
