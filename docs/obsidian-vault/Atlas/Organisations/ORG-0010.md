*The name under which a group of Black abolitionists in London, among them Olaudah Equiano and Ottobah Cugoano, signed letters and addresses against the slave trade in the later 1780s.*

The Sons of Africa were a group of African abolitionists in London in the later 1780s. Adi and Sherwood call them "the Black abolitionists in London, who began to style themselves the Sons of Africa".[^1] The records consulted show them as signatories rather than as a formal society. They signed letters of thanks to abolitionists, letters to the press and appeals to politicians.[^2][^3] [Olaudah Equiano](Atlas/People/PER-0006.md), who signed as Gustavus Vassa, was among their leaders. Ottobah Cugoano was another.[^1][^2]

## Founding and founders

No founding meeting, date or founding document appears in the sources consulted. Adi and Sherwood write that from 1786 Cugoano's name appears "alongside other 'Sons of Africa'" in letters to abolitionists, politicians and others in the campaign.[^4] Carretta sees the name itself as new. Only towards the end of the eighteenth century, he writes, did some Africans removed by the slave trade begin to adopt a shared public identity as Africans, "calling themselves 'Sons of Africa' in both Britain and America".[^5]

## Members

Fryer names those who signed with Equiano and Cugoano a letter from nine "Sons of Africa" published in *The Diary* in 1789. They were Yahne Aelane (Joseph Sanders), Boughwa Gegansmel or Broughwar Jugensmel (Jasper Goree), Cojoh Ammere (George Williams), Thomas Cooper, William Green, George Robert Mandeville and Bernard Elliot Griffiths.[^2] He lists more than a dozen others who signed public statements as Sons of Africa "at various times in 1787–8", including Daniel and John Christopher, James Forster and Thomas Jones.[^2]

## Aims

Their letters supported the campaign against the slave trade and slavery. Signing their address of thanks to Granville Sharp in December 1787, they described themselves as "we, who are a part, or descendants, of the much-wronged people of Africa".[^3] Carretta concludes that for Equiano and his colleagues one was "as much a 'Son of Africa' by descent as by birth".[^3]

## Addresses, letters and delegations

- **Address to the Quakers, October 1785.** In his *Narrative* Equiano writes: "In October 1785 I was accompanied by some of the Africans, and presented this address of thanks to the gentlemen called Friends or Quakers, in Gracechurch-Court Lombard-Street." He prints the address in full. It thanks the Quakers for their book *A Caution to Great Britain and her Colonies* and for their "kind interposition, towards breaking the yoke of slavery". It is offered by those who are "a part of those captived, oppressed, and afflicted people". Equiano adds: "These gentlemen received us very kindly, with a promise to exert themselves on behalf of the oppressed Africans, and we parted."[^6] Walvin dates the letter to 21 October 1785. It was handed over "by Gustavus Vassa and Seven others", and Walvin infers that there had been a meeting, or meetings, of Africans to draft it.[^7] The address predates the earliest use of the name "Sons of Africa" found in the sources consulted. Adi and Sherwood, however, place it among the group's activities.[^1]
- **Address of thanks to Granville Sharp, 15 December 1787.** Equiano, as Gustavus Vassa, and nine other men signed "The Address of Thanks of the Sons of Africa to the Honourable Granville Sharp, Esq." Only Cugoano and one other signatory used both their African and their slave names.[^3]
- **Dolben's Bill, 1788.** Adi and Sherwood write that Equiano regularly consulted Sir William Dolben, whose Bill to regulate the slave trade became law in 1788, and "led several delegations of the Sons of Africa to the Houses of Parliament".[^1] Fryer is more cautious. It was "no doubt these leaders, or some of them", he writes, who went with Equiano to Westminster to hear the debates and the examination of witnesses.[^2]
- **Letters to the press.** In 1789 nine Sons of Africa, Equiano and Cugoano among them, published a letter in *The Diary*. It declared that "thanks to God the nation at large is awakened to a sense of our sufferings, except the Oran Otang philosophers", by which they meant the pro-slavery pamphleteers.[^2] Fryer notes that the letters signed by the Sons of Africa are collected in Folarin Shyllon's *Black People in Britain* (1977).[^8]

## Links with people in the atlas

- **[Olaudah Equiano](Atlas/People/PER-0006.md)** was one of the group's leaders and signed its letters as Gustavus Vassa.[^1][^3] Fryer describes him as "one of the acknowledged political leaders of his fellow-blacks", and as "not by any means the only leader".[^2]

## Fate

The sources consulted do not record what became of the group or when it stopped using the name. Its known public statements fall between 1786 and 1789.[^2][^4]

## Evidence notes

- **Organisation.** The sources consulted give no rules, officers, meeting place or membership list. The "Sons of Africa" are known from the signatures on their letters.
- **Delegations to Parliament.** Adi and Sherwood state that Equiano led delegations of the Sons of Africa to Parliament. Fryer infers that some of the signatories went with him.[^1][^2]
- **Letters not consulted.** The newspaper texts of the 1787–89 letters, and Shyllon's collection of them, were not consulted for this edition. The quotations from the Sharp address and the 1789 letter come from Carretta and Fryer.
