*The membership organisation of the Chartist movement, founded at Manchester in July 1840 and wound up in 1860; William Cuffay represented Westminster on its London district council from 1841 and was the council's treasurer from 1842.*

<!-- MEDIA: image slot — a National Charter Association membership card of the 1840s (the card showed a male and a female worker); to be selected and added by the editor -->

The National Charter Association of Great Britain (NCA) was the organisation through which the Chartists campaigned for the People's Charter from 1840. Malcolm Chase calls it "highly innovatory, the first national political party in history".[^1] Its London district council was the body on which [William Cuffay](Atlas/People/PER-0009.md) served. As its delegate he represented Westminster and later acted as its treasurer.[^2]

## Founding

The NCA was launched at a conference of twenty-three delegates in Manchester. The Chartist Ancestors history dates the opening to 20 July 1840, at the Griffin Inn on Great Ancoats Street.[^1][^3] Chase writes that its constitution was "an amalgam of elements from virtually all the plans that had been advanced over the preceding months, except O'Connor's". Only three members of the 1839 Convention took part.[^4] Feargus O'Connor, Chartism's leading figure, was in prison at York when it was formed.[^4][^3] Chase describes James Leach, a Manchester mill worker and president of the provisional executive, as "Britain's first, formally elected national political leader".[^4]

## Aims and structure

Membership was open to anyone who subscribed and agreed with its object, which was "to obtain a 'Radical Reform' of the House of Commons, in other words, a full and faithful Representation of the entire people of the United Kingdom", on the principles of the People's Charter.[^1] The constitution organised members into "classes of ten persons", each paying a penny a week. Each town was to have a Chartist council, each region a county or district council, and the whole was governed by an elected national executive council.[^4] The NCA's membership card showed a male and a female worker side by side. Chase notes that the Charter itself asked only for universal male suffrage, but that the NCA "made no distinction between the membership rights" of women and men.[^1]

At its peak the NCA had some 400 branches and about 50,000 members. Chase cautions that it "always appeared more coherent and comprehensive on paper than on the ground".[^1]

## London

The Metropolitan Charter Union, Chase writes, "overcame its disdain to become the NCA's London district council in September 1840".[^1] Cuffay helped form the London tailors' Chartist locality. He was Westminster's representative on the metropolitan district council from 1841 and its treasurer from 1842.[^2] Fryer describes how, in 1842, the Metropolitan Delegate Council answered the arrest of George Julian Harney and other national leaders. It appointed Cuffay, as president, and three others as an interim executive "to supply the place of those whom a tyrannic Government has pounced upon".[^5] According to the Chartist Ancestors history, the national executive moved to London in 1843.[^3]

## Campaigns

- **Petitions.** The NCA organised the national petitions of 1842 and 1848, and smaller ones later.[^3] The 1848 petition was brought to London by a National Convention that assembled on 4 April. A mass meeting on [Kennington Common on 10 April](Atlas/Events/EVT-0012.md) was to escort it to Parliament. Cuffay chaired the Demonstration Committee that organised the processions.[^6]
- **Elections.** Its constitution commended Bronterre O'Brien's plan to put forward Chartist candidates at elections.[^4] In 1847 the NCA set up a central body to coordinate the election of Chartist MPs.[^3]

## Publications

The *Northern Star*, O'Connor's newspaper, was the main channel between the executive and the members. Chase writes that "Without the Star, the NCA and its executive would have been almost paralysed". The executive's own periodical of autumn 1841 ran to only four issues.[^7]

## The conspiracy of 1848

Some London Chartists, Cuffay among them, turned to conspiracy in the summer of 1848. It ended with the police raid on the [Orange Tree](Atlas/Events/EVT-0013.md) in August.[^8] Chase states that this "revived conspiracy lacked any direct link to the NCA executive".[^9] Cuffay was convicted at the Old Bailey in September 1848 ([see the trial](Atlas/Events/EVT-0014.md)) and transported for life.[^10]

## Fate

Membership fell steeply after 1848, perhaps to as few as 500. At its London convention in March 1851 the NCA adopted a socialist programme, "the Charter and something more". Ernest Jones kept it going as "a one-man executive committee". A final convention met in 1858, and the NCA was formally wound up two years later.[^11]

## Evidence notes

- **Name of the London body.** Chase calls the London body the NCA's "metropolitan district council"; Fryer calls it the "Metropolitan Delegate Council".[^2][^5]
- **The 1842 interim executive.** Fryer records Cuffay's appointment as president of the interim executive. Chase writes that he "helped rescue the national executive when it faced crisis late in 1842".[^5][^2]
- **Founding date.** Chase's account consulted here does not give the day of the Manchester conference; the date of 20 July 1840 comes from the Chartist Ancestors history.[^3]
