*The International African Service Bureau, launched in London in 1937 by George Padmore, C. L. R. James, I. T. A. Wallace-Johnson, Ras Makonnen and others to inform British opinion about the colonies and press for African self-determination; it joined the Pan-African Federation in 1944.*

The International African Service Bureau for the Defence of Africans and People of African Descent (IASB) was launched in London in 1937. It grew out of the [International African Friends of Abyssinia](Atlas/Organisations/ORG-0008.md).[^1][^2] It was small and independent of every European party. Fryer describes its members' Pan-Africanism as "an independent political expression of Negro aspirations for complete national independence from white domination – Capitalist or Communist".[^2]

## Founding

After the Abyssinian campaign, [George Padmore](Atlas/People/PER-0005.md) tried from about June 1936 to launch a broader "Pan-African Federation". Its public meeting at the [Memorial Hall, Farringdon Street](Atlas/Places/PLC-0041.md), on 31 July 1936 had Labour and Independent Labour Party speakers on the platform.[^3] Ras Makonnen rented a basement flat at 2 Calthorpe Street as a meeting place.[^3] The arrival in London of the Sierra Leonean trade unionist I. T. A. Wallace-Johnson, in Britain for his sedition appeal, was decisive. Høgsbjerg dates the launch of the IASB to May 1937; Fryer writes that it replaced the IAFA in March 1937.[^1][^2]

## Aims

The first issue of its bulletin, *Africa and the World* (July 1937), set out its purpose:

> It supports the demands of Africans and other colonial peoples for democratic rights, civil liberties and self-determination. One of the chief functions of the Bureau will be to help and enlighten public opinion in Great Britain, especially the working and middle classes, as to the true condition in the various colonies …[^1]

## Leadership and members

Padmore was chair, [C. L. R. James](Atlas/People/PER-0014.md) editorial director, Wallace-Johnson general secretary and Makonnen treasurer and fund-raiser.[^1][^2] [Amy Ashwood Garvey](Atlas/People/PER-0004.md) was vice-president.[^4] Fryer records that [Jomo Kenyatta](Atlas/People/PER-0017.md) worked closely with them in founding it.[^5] Makonnen's constitution limited "Active Membership" to "Africans and peoples of African descent". Sympathisers of other races could become associate members.[^1][^4] Its patrons included Nancy Cunard, Victor Gollancz, Sylvia Pankhurst, D. N. Pritt, Ellen Wilkinson and Arthur Creech Jones.[^4][^2] Makonnen recalled that it "came together in an informal way … there wasn't any clear membership".[^1]

## Meetings and places

The Bureau held regular open-air meetings on Sunday afternoons in Hyde Park.[^1] Its first quarterly meeting, on 7 September 1937, was held in an Indian restaurant. By then it had prompted MPs to ask twenty-three questions in Parliament since May.[^1] Its patrons' support enabled it to take offices on Gray's Inn Road.[^4]

## Publications

- *Africa and the World* (1937), co-edited by James.[^1]
- The *African Sentinel*, launched by Wallace-Johnson in October 1937.[^1] When he returned to Sierra Leone in April 1938, Freetown customs seized 2,000 copies containing an article by Kenyatta.[^5]
- [*International African Opinion*](Atlas/Works/WRK-0013.md), edited by James and sold at Hyde Park meetings. Its motto was "Educate, Co-operate, Emancipate: Neutral in nothing affecting the African Peoples".[^6]

## Campaigns and alliances

The Bureau sent speakers to Labour Party and trade-union branches and co-operative guilds.[^2] With the League of Coloured Peoples and the Negro Welfare Association, it submitted a joint memorandum to the Moyne Commission on the West Indies.[^7] It worked with radical Sinhalese and Burmese students in London.[^6]

## Fate

The Bureau was much less active in the early war years, and a quarrel over money led to a breakaway by its general secretary, Edward Sigismund. Special Branch nonetheless judged that its policy would "remain in the hands of the Trotskyist, George Padmore".[^6] Towards the end of 1944 it joined other Black organisations to form the Pan-African Federation. The Federation organised the [Fifth Pan-African Congress](Atlas/Events/EVT-0004.md) in 1945, at which the Bureau was represented by Padmore, Makonnen, Nkrumah, Peter Abrahams and "Mrs Garvey".[^6][^8]

## Evidence notes

- **Launch date.** The Bureau's launch is dated to May 1937 by Høgsbjerg and to March 1937 by Fryer.
- **Location of the offices.** The house number of the Gray's Inn Road offices is not given in the sources consulted.

## Connected people

- **[Amy Ashwood Garvey](Atlas/People/PER-0004.md)** — Amy Ashwood Garvey was among the founders of the International African Service Bureau in 1937 and its vice-president.[^9]
- **[George Padmore](Atlas/People/PER-0005.md)** — George Padmore was a founder and the chair of the International African Service Bureau, launched in London in 1937.[^10]
- **[Constance Cummings-John](Atlas/People/PER-0015.md)** — Constance Cummings-John was a member of the International African Service Bureau in London in the 1930s.[^11]
