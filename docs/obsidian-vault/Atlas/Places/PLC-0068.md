28 Penton Street, Islington, was the London headquarters of the [African National Congress](Atlas/Organisations/ORG-0002.md) (ANC) while the movement was banned in South Africa. The Anti-Apartheid Legacy centre, which gives its own address as 28 Penton Street, calls it "the UK headquarters of the then exiled African National Congress".[^1] The Anti-Apartheid Movement's archives committee recorded that [Oliver Tambo](Atlas/People/PER-0020.md), President of the ANC, "was known ... to hold early morning meetings at the ANC Mission in Penton Street" when he was in London.[^2]

## The bombing, 14 March 1982

At 9 a.m. on Sunday 14 March 1982 a bomb exploded against the rear wall of the building ([the bombing of the ANC office](Atlas/Events/EVT-0021.md)).[^1] The only person inside, the volunteer Vernet Mbatha, was slightly injured and escaped across the neighbouring roofs.[^1] The blast destroyed records and office equipment, and the ANC's printing press was thrown through a wall. The ANC printer Patric Tariq Mellet recalled that the press "was repairable and was soon set to work again".[^1] The Anti-Apartheid Legacy centre suggests that Tambo may have been the target. He was due to speak at a rally in Trafalgar Square later that day.[^1]

At a Truth and Reconciliation Commission amnesty hearing in February 1999, Gill Marcus, who worked at 28 Penton Street, testified that she was usually in the office every Sunday morning, "except the day of the bomb".[^3] Nine former South African security agents admitted responsibility for the attack to the Commission.[^1]

## Rebuilding and later years

While the building was rebuilt, the British Council of Churches lent the ANC office space. Archbishop Trevor Huddleston, President of the Anti-Apartheid Movement, launched an "ANC Office Appeal Fund".[^1] After the rebuilding, the Anti-Apartheid Legacy centre writes, "the ANC returned to Penton Street". It continued to coordinate campaigns there, including "Votes for Freedom" before the elections of 1994.[^1] After the elections the ANC "no longer needed their Islington base". Action for Southern Africa, the successor of the Anti-Apartheid Movement, used the premises from 1994 until 2008.[^1] According to the same account, "A green heritage plaque was installed on the building in 2010 to commemorate the ANC's 16 years in Penton Street".[^1]

## People connected with the building

- **[Oliver Tambo](Atlas/People/PER-0020.md)**, President of the ANC, used the mission when he was in London.[^2] The sources consulted do not record a visit to the building by [Nelson Mandela](Atlas/People/PER-0002.md).

## Evidence notes

- **Dates of occupation.** The plaque's "16 years", as reported by the Anti-Apartheid Legacy centre, would place the ANC's arrival at Penton Street around 1978. The sources consulted do not give the date of arrival directly.[^1]
- **The bomb.** Its weight is given as 11 lb by the Anti-Apartheid Legacy centre and as 10 lb by the Anti-Apartheid Movement's submission to the Truth and Reconciliation Commission.[^1][^2]
- **Tambo as the target.** This is the Anti-Apartheid Legacy centre's inference ("It is likely that…"), not an established finding.[^1]
- **The building today.** The Anti-Apartheid Legacy centre gives its own address as 28 Penton Street. The plaque reported in its account was not checked against an official record for this edition.[^1]

## Connected people

- **[Oliver Tambo](Atlas/People/PER-0020.md)** — Oliver Tambo used the ANC's London mission at 28 Penton Street, holding early-morning meetings there when he was in London.[^4]
