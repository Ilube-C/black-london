## At a glance
Oliver Reginald Kaizana Tambo (27 October 1917 – 23 April 1993) was a South African anti-apartheid leader who led the [African National Congress](Atlas/Organisations/ORG-0002.md) from exile, as acting president after Chief Albert Luthuli's death and later as its President.[^1][^2] Sent abroad by the ANC in 1960 to build its external mission, he made London one of his bases, and his family settled there; [Nelson Mandela](Atlas/People/PER-0002.md) visited him in London in [1962](Atlas/Events/EVT-0005.md).[^1][^3]

## Life and significance
Shortly before the 1960 State of Emergency that followed Sharpeville, Tambo left South Africa on the ANC's instructions.[^2] Callinicos writes that the Sharpeville killings sped up the ANC's plan for an external mission, and that Tambo had to leave at once, before he could be arrested.[^1] Later that year he flew on to London from Scandinavia and stayed with Canon John Collins and his wife Diana; his wife Adelaide and their children reached London on 15 September 1960, shortly before he left for the United Nations.[^1]

The family's later home was a house in [Muswell Hill, Haringey](Atlas/Places/PLC-0067.md), for which Adelaide Tambo raised the deposit after some fifteen years of working double shifts in hospitals, with help from anti-apartheid organisations.[^1] Tambo, usually travelling, was seldom there, and dissidents in the ANC used the house and his children's schooling against him.[^1] Visitors from all over the world spent days or weeks at the house, and students and other young people sometimes stayed for months.[^1]

In 1962, during a secret tour abroad, Nelson Mandela visited Tambo, then the ANC's external leader, in London.[^3] Mandela spent ten days in London, partly to see Tambo, whose health worried him, and Tambo's wife Adelaide and their children; with Mary Benson, Tambo and Mandela toured Westminster Abbey, Big Ben and the Houses of Parliament.[^2] In London Mandela also had a long talk with Adelaide Tambo.[^1] In the Commons tributes of December 2013 Gordon Brown dated Mandela's first visit to London to June 1962 and recalled him there "with his friend Oliver Tambo".[^4] When Chief Albert Luthuli died in July 1967, Tambo, as acting president-general, was the man who could fill his place at the head of the ANC.[^2] Tambo insisted that he was only the Acting President, and remained so for some ten years after Luthuli's death; the National Executive Committee then elected him President, a step he protested had been taken without a mandate from the membership.[^1] The ANC's London Mission, its UK headquarters, was at [28 Penton Street](Atlas/Places/PLC-0068.md) in Islington, and when Tambo was in London he held early-morning meetings there.[^5][^6][^7] At 9 a.m. on Sunday 14 March 1982 [a bomb exploded](Atlas/Events/EVT-0021.md) at the rear of the building, injuring the ANC researcher Vernet Mbatha. It was the morning of an Anti-Apartheid Movement rally in Trafalgar Square at which Tambo had been billed to speak, though he was in fact at a Front-line States meeting in Maputo.[^5][^6] Nine former agents of the apartheid security services later admitted to the Truth and Reconciliation Commission that they had planned and carried out the attack.[^5] Tambo returned to South Africa in December 1990 after three decades in exile.[^2][^1] He died in the early hours of 23 April 1993, after a heart attack the night before.[^1]

## Evidence notes
1. **Date of death:** Callinicos gives the early hours of Friday 23 April 1993.[^1]
2. **His title:** *Long Walk to Freedom* calls him acting president-general after Luthuli's death in 1967;[^2] Callinicos says that the National Executive Committee later elected him President, and Thabo Mbeki's foreword says that Tambo always insisted he was merely the Acting President.[^1]
3. **Muswell Hill:** Callinicos does not date the purchase of the house, which came after Adelaide Tambo's years of hospital work, or give its street address, so the house is not dated here.[^1]
4. **28 Penton Street:** The sources examined place the ANC's London mission at 28 Penton Street by the time of the 1982 bombing but do not say when it moved there.[^5][^6]
