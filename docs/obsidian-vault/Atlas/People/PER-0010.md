## At a glance
Samuel Coleridge-Taylor (15 August 1875 – 1 September 1912) was a London-born composer and conductor of Sierra Leonean and English parentage who achieved international acclaim for *[Hiawatha's Wedding Feast](Atlas/Works/WRK-0007.md)*.[^1] He attended the 1900 [First Pan-African Conference](Atlas/Events/EVT-0001.md).[^2]

## Life and significance
Samuel Coleridge-Taylor was born on 15 August 1875 at [15 Theobald's Road, Red Lion Square, Holborn](Atlas/Places/PLC-0029.md), London.[^1] He began studying at the [Royal College of Music](Atlas/Organisations/ORG-0015.md) in the Christmas Term of 1890 and left at the end of the Easter Term of 1897.[^1] On [11 November 1898](Atlas/Events/EVT-0015.md), a Royal College of Music concert conducted by Sir Charles Stanford gave the first performance of his cantata *Hiawatha's Wedding Feast*.[^1]

He attended the First Pan-African Conference in London in July 1900,[^2] held at [Westminster Town Hall](Atlas/Places/PLC-0001.md); according to a 2014 article he provided the conference's music and later joined "the committee".[^3] Fryer identifies the committee: at the conference the [African Association](Atlas/Organisations/ORG-0001.md) was merged into a new Pan-African Association, and Coleridge-Taylor was elected to its executive committee.[^4] The Black History Month account calls him the youngest delegate.[^5] [W. E. B. Du Bois](Atlas/People/PER-0001.md) first saw Coleridge-Taylor at a gathering in London and later visited his home, a friendship he commemorated in 'The Immortal Child' (*Darkwater*, ch. VIII).[^6] He had read Du Bois's *The Souls of Black Folk* by January 1904, writing that it was "about the finest book I have ever read by a coloured man, and one of the best by any author, white or black".[^1] His [*Twenty-Four Negro Melodies*](Atlas/Works/WRK-0008.md), transcribed for the piano with a preface by Booker T. Washington, was published in Boston by Oliver Ditson in 1905.[^7]

## Evidence notes
1. **Du Bois and the *Negro Melodies*:** The composer's foreword to *Twenty-Four Negro Melodies*, dated London, 17 December 1904, does not mention Du Bois or *The Souls of Black Folk*. It acknowledges Henri Junod's *Les Chants et les Contes des Ba Ronga*, Mrs Victoria Randall and Frederick J. Loudin of the Fisk Jubilee Singers.[^7] His admiration for *The Souls of Black Folk* is documented in his letter of 3 January 1904.[^1] No source examined says that the book shaped his music.
2. **The college building:** When he entered the Royal College of Music in 1890 it occupied its original building, which Sayers describes as later the home of the Royal College of Organists.[^1] The sources examined do not say when the college moved to its present building, so no modern address is given for it here.
3. **When he and Du Bois met:** Du Bois wrote that it was "nineteen years" since he had first seen Coleridge-Taylor at a gathering in London, but gives no year;[^6] the meeting is therefore not dated here.
