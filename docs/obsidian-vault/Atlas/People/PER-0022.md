## At a glance
Stuart McPhail Hall (3 February 1932 – 10 February 2014) was a Jamaican-born cultural theorist whose work reshaped modern understandings of race and diaspora identity.[^1] Moving to London in 1957 (1958 in his memoir),[^1][^2] he was the first editor of the *[New Left Review](Atlas/Works/WRK-0025.md)* (1960–1961).[^3][^4] At the time of the 1958 racial violence he became active, through the New Left, in anti-racist work in [Notting Hill](Atlas/Places/PLC-0072.md).[^2]

## Life and significance
Born in Kingston, Jamaica, Hall left for Britain in 1951 as Jamaica's Rhodes scholar, read English at Merton College, Oxford, graduated in 1954 and began a DPhil on Henry James that he never completed; in 1957 he moved to London.[^1] His memoir dates the move to 1958.[^2] He taught English at [a secondary modern school in Stockwell](Atlas/Places/PLC-0071.md) and later lectured in film and mass media studies at Chelsea College.[^1] In the memoir he describes the job as supply teaching at Stockwell Secondary Modern School, where about a quarter of the boys were black and came from around Brixton and the Oval; in the afternoons he went up to Soho to edit *Universities and Left Review*.[^2] In 1958 Hall, Raphael Samuel and Eric Hobsbawm founded the Partisan Coffee House at [7 Carlisle Street](Atlas/Places/PLC-0070.md), Soho, with a library and the offices of *Universities and Left Review* upstairs.[^4] In Hall's own account the café was Samuel's project: an editorial meeting voted against it, but Samuel went ahead and raised the funds.[^2] When that journal merged with *The New Reasoner* in January 1960 to form the *New Left Review*, Hall became its first editor; Perry Anderson succeeded him in 1962.[^3][^4]

The 1958 riots in Notting Hill and Nottingham marked the start of another of his lasting concerns: how Britain had responded to colonial migration.[^1] Hall recalls that the ULR Club worked in Notting Hill with local black activists, churches and left-wingers in the local Labour Party to put together local black and white tenants' defence associations, its first experience of community politics.[^2] His memoir also mentions 'the house we bought together in [Kilburn](Atlas/Places/PLC-0073.md)', where Catherine Hall's mother later had her own flat.[^2] He chaired both [Iniva (the Institute of International Visual Arts) and Autograph (the Association of Black Photographers)](Atlas/Organisations/ORG-0029.md), the two bodies that commissioned Rivington Place in [Shoreditch](Atlas/Places/PLC-0074.md), and was the central figure in its creation; it opened in 2007.[^1] He died on 10 February 2014 at St John's Hospice, Westminster.[^1]

## Connections

- **[C. L. R. James](Atlas/People/PER-0014.md)** — Stuart Hall held a conversation with C. L. R. James that was later published, and wrote a portrait of him.[^5]
- **[Partisan Coffee House](Atlas/Places/PLC-0070.md)** — Stuart Hall edited Universities and Left Review at its Soho offices above the Partisan Coffee House, 7 Carlisle Street, in the late 1950s.[^6]
- **[Notting Hill](Atlas/Places/PLC-0072.md)** — Stuart Hall took part in anti-racist work in Notting Hill after the riots of 1958, through the New Left's Universities and Left Review Club.[^7]
- **[New Left Review](Atlas/Works/WRK-0025.md)** — Stuart Hall was the first editor of New Left Review, in 1960–61.[^8]

## Evidence notes
1. **The move to London:** The Oxford DNB dates it to 1957;[^1] Hall's memoir says that he left Oxford for London in 1958.[^2]
2. **Home:** The memoir names the house in Kilburn that he and Catherine Hall bought;[^2] no other London home of his is confirmed in the sources examined.
3. **School:** Both sources name a secondary modern school in Stockwell.[^2][^1]
