## At a glance
William Cuffay (1788 – 1870) was a Black British tailor who became a leading London Chartist in the 1840s: Westminster's representative on the [National Charter Association](Atlas/Organisations/ORG-0014.md)'s metropolitan district council from 1841 and its treasurer from 1842.[^1] He died in July 1870, aged 82, in the sick ward of the Brickfields invalid depot in Tasmania.[^2] He was convicted at the [Old Bailey](Atlas/Places/PLC-0028.md) in September 1848 of feloniously compassing to levy war against the Queen and sentenced to transportation for life;[^3] he was transported to Tasmania.[^4]

## Life and significance
William Cuffay was born in Gillingham, Kent, in 1788, the son of Juliana Fox and Chatham Cuffay, a formerly enslaved worker at Chatham Dockyard; he moved to London in 1819.[^4] Malcolm Chase gives a different account: his father had been born into slavery on St Kitts; his parents sailed from St Kitts in 1788 while his mother was heavily pregnant; his Chartist colleagues believed he had been born at sea, but in old age he said he had been born in Chatham, the dockyard town where the family settled; and his father enlisted as a cook in the Royal Navy.[^1] Fryer also gives Chatham and 1788, and says his father found work as a cook on a warship.[^2] A tailor, Cuffay helped form the London tailors' Chartist locality;[^1] Fryer says that in the autumn of 1839 he helped set up the Metropolitan Tailors' Charter Association, which about eighty people joined on its first night.[^2]

In April 1848 Cuffay was a delegate to the National Chartist Convention, which assembled on 4 April, and chaired the 'Demonstration Committee' that organised the processions to and from the [10 April meeting on Kennington Common](Atlas/Events/EVT-0012.md); he was among the delegates on the platform there.[^1] Fryer calls him one of London's three delegates to the Convention and chairman of the committee managing the procession.[^2]

On the evening of 18 August 1848, two days after police arrested the men meeting at the [Orange Tree public house](Atlas/Places/PLC-0027.md) in Orange Street, Red Lion Square, Holborn, Cuffay was taken into custody at his lodging at 11 Hollins Street, Wardour Street, Soho.[^3] Tried with William Lacey and Thomas Fay at the Old Bailey in the September 1848 session, he was found guilty, aged 60, and sentenced to transportation for life.[^3] He stayed in Tasmania, remaining involved in politics; he died poor, aged 82, and seven Australian newspapers published obituaries.[^4] Chase adds that his wife Mary joined him in 1853, that once paroled he worked as a tailor, that he and Mary chose to stay after all political prisoners were pardoned in 1856, and that he died in 1870 in the Brickfield Invalid Depot.[^1]

## Evidence notes
1. **Birthplace and father:** The education pack gives Gillingham and describes his father as a formerly enslaved worker at Chatham Dockyard.[^4] Chase says that his father was born into slavery on St Kitts and later enlisted as a naval cook, that his colleagues thought William had been born at sea, and that he himself later named Chatham.[^1] Both accounts are given above.
2. **Sentence:** The same pack says that he was sentenced to 21 years' transportation and pardoned three years later.[^4] The Old Bailey record gives "Transported for Life",[^3] and Chase agrees and dates his pardon to 1856, when all political prisoners were pardoned;[^1] the record and Chase are followed here. The pack does not name its publisher.
3. **The National Charter Association:** Launched at a conference in Manchester on 20 July 1840, it was the largest and longest-lived Chartist membership body.[^8]
