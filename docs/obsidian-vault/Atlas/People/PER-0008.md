## At a glance
Mary Prince (born c. 1788) was a Bermudian woman enslaved in Bermuda, Turks Island, and Antigua who claimed her freedom in London in 1828 and produced one of the earliest published narratives by a Black woman in Britain, [*The History of Mary Prince*](Atlas/Works/WRK-0006.md) (1831).[^1][^2] Busby calls her the first Black British woman to escape from slavery and publish a record of her experiences; the *History* was published in London and Edinburgh in 1831 and reached a third edition that year.[^2] Her narrative led to two libel actions in the London courts in 1833.[^1][^3]

## Life and significance
Born enslaved in Bermuda around 1788, Mary Prince was brought to Antigua, where she joined the Moravian Church. In 1828 she travelled with her enslavers, the Woods, to London, lodging at [Leigh Street](Atlas/Places/PLC-0021.md) in Bloomsbury.[^1]

Two or three months after they arrived, the Woods turned Prince out of their London lodging. She took her trunk to the Moravian missionaries at Hatton Garden, where she saw a Mr Moore, and lodged with Mash, the family's shoe-black, and his wife. About the end of November 1828 she went to the [Anti-Slavery Society](Atlas/Organisations/ORG-0013.md)'s office in [Aldermanbury](Atlas/Places/PLC-0023.md); the *History*'s title page gives No. 18 Aldermanbury as the address of the Society's clerk, Joseph Phillips.[^1]

Prince entered the service of the Anti-Slavery Society's secretary, Thomas Pringle, and his wife.[^1] Vigne says she moved in with them in Pentonville as their domestic servant in 1829, and places their home in [Solly Terrace, Pentonville](Atlas/Places/PLC-0024.md), until March 1833.[^4] She dictated her life story to Susanna Strickland, a friend of Pringle's who lodged with the family,[^4] and it was published as *The History of Mary Prince* in 1831.[^1] Two libel trials followed in 1833.[^3] In the first, *Pringle v. Cadell*, Pringle sued the publisher Thomas Cadell; it was heard in the Court of Common Pleas on 21 February, and Prince was called only briefly, to confirm that she had dictated her account, and was not cross-examined.[^3] In the second, *Wood v. Pringle*, her former enslaver John Adams Wood sued Pringle; it was heard in the [Court of King's Bench](Atlas/Places/PLC-0025.md) on 27 February, and Prince was the first witness for the defence. Her testimony was reported at length in the press, including *The Times* of 1 March 1833.[^3] Salih's notes date this second case to March 1833.[^1]

## Connections

- **[Leigh Street, Bloomsbury](Atlas/Places/PLC-0021.md)** — Mary Prince stayed with her enslavers, John and Margaret Wood, at their lodgings in Leigh Street, Bloomsbury, after arriving in London in 1828.[^5]
- **[Solly Terrace, Pentonville](Atlas/Places/PLC-0024.md)** — Mary Prince lived with Thomas and Susan Pringle in Solly Terrace, Pentonville, as their domestic servant from 1829.[^6]
- **[Court of Common Pleas Trial (Pringle v. Cadell)](Atlas/Events/EVT-0010.md)** — Mary Prince was briefly called as a witness in the libel trial Pringle v. Cadell in the Court of Common Pleas on 21 February 1833.[^7]

## Evidence notes
1. **Her later life:** No record of Mary Prince after her evidence in *Wood v. Pringle*, reported in *The Times* on 1 March 1833, has been found in the sources examined, and her date of death is unknown.[^3]
2. **Hatton Garden:** The *History* says that she took her trunk to the Moravian missionaries at Hatton Garden.[^1] No source examined places her at the Moravian chapel in Fetter Lane.
3. **The two courts:** Lambert shows that the two cases were heard in different courts: *Pringle v. Cadell* in the Court of Common Pleas on 21 February 1833, and *Wood v. Pringle* in the Court of King's Bench on 27 February 1833.[^3] Salih's notes date the second case to March 1833.[^1]
4. **The Pringles' address:** Vigne places the Pringles' home in Solly Terrace, Pentonville, until March 1833, and that address is used here.[^4]
