## At a glance
Olaudah Equiano (c. 1745 – 31 March 1797), known throughout most of his life in Britain as Gustavus Vassa, was a formerly enslaved seafarer, commissary, and writer who became a prominent Black voice in the late eighteenth-century British abolitionist movement in London.[^1][^2] Baptised at St Margaret's Church, [Westminster](Atlas/Places/PLC-0015.md) in February 1759, he later became one of the leaders of the [Sons of Africa](Atlas/Organisations/ORG-0010.md), the Black abolitionists in London,[^3] and published his bestselling autobiography, *The [Interesting Narrative of the Life of Olaudah Equiano](Atlas/Works/WRK-0003.md)*, from his Marylebone lodging at [No. 10 Union Street](Atlas/Places/PLC-0016.md), Middlesex Hospital in 1789.[^1][^2]

## Life and significance
Enslaved as a child, he was renamed Gustavus Vassa by the Royal Navy officer Michael Henry Pascal.[^1][^3] According to his *Narrative*, he arrived in England in spring 1757, aged about twelve;[^1] Carretta dates his first arrival to mid-December 1754 from naval and other records.[^4] On 9 February 1759, he was [baptised](Atlas/Events/EVT-0008.md) at St Margaret's Church, Westminster, where the parish register recorded him as "Gustavus Vassa a Black born in Carolina 12 years old".[^4] After purchasing his freedom in 1766, Equiano returned repeatedly to London between maritime voyages across the Atlantic.[^1]

In the 1780s Equiano became one of the leaders of the Black abolitionists in London: in 1785 he and other Africans delivered an address to the Quakers thanking them for their abolitionist work, and he worked closely with leading abolitionists, consulted Sir William Dolben over his 1788 bill to regulate the slave trade, and led several delegations of the Sons of Africa to the Houses of Parliament.[^3] On 19 March 1783 he alerted the abolitionist Granville Sharp to the *Zong* massacre, as Sharp's diary records.[^2] Appointed Commissary of Stores in November 1786 for the government-backed Sierra Leone resettlement scheme in London, he was dismissed in March 1787 after, by his own account, exposing corruption in the scheme.[^1][^4] With other Black Londoners in the Sons of Africa,[^3] Equiano published anti-slavery letters in London newspapers; he and Ottobah Cugoano both signed such letters.[^2]

Living at No. 10 Union Street, Middlesex Hospital, Equiano self-financed and published *The Interesting Narrative* in London in 1789, raising the money in advance from subscribers.[^1][^2] In his final years in London, after his wife Susanna was buried in February 1796, he drafted his will;[^2] Carretta dates it to 28 May 1796, when he was living in [Plasterers' Hall](Atlas/Places/PLC-0017.md), Addle Street, in the City of London.[^4] He died in London on 31 March 1797[^2] and was buried on 6 April 1797 at Whitefield's Tabernacle burial ground on [Tottenham Court Road](Atlas/Places/PLC-0018.md).[^4]

## Evidence notes
1. **Place of birth:** In the first chapter of the *Narrative* Equiano says that he was born in Essaka, in Igboland (in present-day Nigeria).[^1] Vincent Carretta, writing in 1999 and 2005, identified two earlier records that give his birthplace as Carolina: the St Margaret's baptismal entry of 9 February 1759 and the Royal Navy muster roll of 1773, which gives "So. Carolina".[^4] Paul E. Lovejoy accepts that these documents point to South Carolina but argues that the linguistic, geographical and cultural details of the *Narrative* leave "little doubt that he was born in Africa, and specifically in Igboland", and that his account of his homeland should be read as memory.[^8] Adi and Sherwood summarise the dispute: by his own account he was born in Essaka, but some modern historians point to evidence of a birth in South Carolina.[^3] Both positions are given here.
